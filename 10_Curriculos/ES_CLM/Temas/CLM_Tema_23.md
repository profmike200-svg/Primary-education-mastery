## 1. Introduction

A fifth-year teacher brings a real **train ticket from London** to class, together with a map of the Underground and a leaflet from a museum. Pupils pass the objects around, find the price, the date and the destination, and soon they are planning an imaginary trip. Later, they open their textbook at a page about travelling, but the activity now has a new meaning: the language on the page has become **real**. This experience shows the importance of choosing, adapting and creating good materials, which is the subject of this topic.

Indeed, materials are the bridge between the curriculum and the pupils. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha promote **learning situations** connected with pupils' lives, the use of **varied resources**, including digital ones, and **Universal Design for Learning**. Moreover, the curriculum gives schools and teachers autonomy to select their materials, which makes it essential to know how to evaluate them.

To develop this topic, I will first explain how curriculum materials are developed. Then I will analyse the criteria for selecting and using textbooks. After that, I will compare authentic and adapted materials and explain how pupils can collaborate in designing materials. Finally, I will refer to diversity and accessibility before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Development of curriculum materials for the English class

### 2.1. Concept and types

To begin with, **curriculum materials** are all the resources that teachers and pupils use to facilitate learning. They can be classified as **printed** materials (textbooks, readers, worksheets), **visual** materials (flashcards, posters, realia), **audio and audiovisual** materials (songs, stories, videos), **digital** materials (apps, platforms, interactive whiteboards) and **manipulative** materials (puppets, games, dice). In Primary, **realia**, real objects such as fruit, toys or clothes, are particularly valuable, because they connect words with concrete experiences. For example, a box of real clothes for a dressing-up game teaches *hat*, *scarf* and *gloves* far more memorably than a picture, because pupils can touch, wear and talk about them.

### 2.2. Principles for developing materials

But what makes a material effective? **Tomlinson (2011)** proposed a set of principles based on research on language acquisition. Among them, good materials should:
- **Achieve impact**, attracting pupils' curiosity and attention.
- **Help learners feel at ease** and develop **confidence**.
- Be **relevant and useful** for learners.
- **Expose learners to language in authentic use**.
- **Draw learners' attention** to linguistic features.
- Provide **opportunities to use** the language for communication.
- Take into account that the positive effects of instruction are usually **delayed**, so language must be recycled.

For example, a worksheet with a funny comic, a clear model and a final task in which pupils create their own speech bubbles follows these principles much better than a list of isolated sentences to complete.

### 2.3. The process of material design

In addition, designing materials is a **cyclical process**: teachers first analyse their pupils' **needs**, then **design** the material, **try it out** in class, and finally **evaluate** and improve it. This reflective process turns teachers into creators, not just consumers, of materials.

## 3. Criteria for selecting and using textbooks

However, most English classes in Spain use a **textbook**, so knowing how to choose and use it is essential. This takes us to the second part of the title.

### 3.1. Advantages and limitations

Textbooks offer clear **advantages**: they provide a structured sequence, save preparation time, include audio and digital components, and give pupils and families a reference. However, they also have **limitations**: they cannot know the needs of a specific group, they may present stereotypes, their tasks may be repetitive, and their topics may be far from pupils' interests. For this reason, the textbook should be seen as a **resource**, not as **the programme** itself.

### 3.2. Selection criteria

To select a textbook, **Cunningsworth (1995)** suggests analysing it systematically. Some key criteria are:
- **Curriculum**: does it develop the six specific competences of the area, including mediation and interculturality?
- **Methodology**: is it communicative, with learning situations and final tasks?
- **Skills**: are listening, speaking, reading and writing integrated and balanced?
- **Language**: is the vocabulary useful and frequent, and is language recycled across units?
- **Culture**: does it show the diversity of the English-speaking world and avoid stereotypes?
- **Diversity**: does it include activities for different levels and needs?
- **Components**: are the audio, digital resources and teacher's guide of good quality?
- **Design**: is it attractive, clear and appropriate for the age of the pupils?

In practice, teachers can use a **checklist** with these criteria to compare several textbooks before the teaching team makes a decision.

### 3.3. Using the textbook

Furthermore, using a textbook well means **selecting** the most useful activities, **adapting** them to the group, **supplementing** them with other materials and **omitting** what is not relevant. For example, if a unit about clothes only offers a matching exercise, the teacher can add a fashion show in which pupils describe their outfits, turning a mechanical activity into a communicative one. Similarly, digital materials that accompany textbooks, such as interactive activities or platforms, should be evaluated with the same criteria: they are only valuable if they offer meaningful practice and useful feedback, not merely attractive animations.

## 4. Authentic and adapted materials

Beyond textbooks, teachers can use authentic and adapted materials, the third element of the title.

### 4.1. Authentic materials

**Authentic materials** are texts created for native speakers, not for language learners, such as picture books, songs, menus, tickets, cereal boxes, weather forecasts or children's websites. **Widdowson (1978)** made an important distinction: a text may be **genuine**, because it comes from the real world, but it only becomes **authentic** for learners when they use it for a real communicative purpose. In other words, a real menu used to order food in a role-play is authentic; the same menu used only to underline nouns is not.

According to the review by **Gilmore (2007)**, authentic materials can increase **motivation** and expose learners to **real language** and **culture**. However, they also have **limitations**: their language may be too difficult, they may contain unknown cultural references and they take time to prepare. Therefore, with young learners, authentic materials must be chosen carefully: picture books, songs, labels and short videos are usually more accessible than newspaper articles. For instance, first-cycle pupils can sort real fruit labels by colour, second-cycle pupils can read a cinema timetable to choose a film, and third-cycle pupils can compare the weather forecast of two English-speaking cities on a children's website.

### 4.2. Adapted materials

When authentic materials are too difficult, teachers can **adapt** them. Adaptation can involve **simplifying** the language, **shortening** the text, **adding visual support**, **elaborating** with explanations or glossaries, or changing the **task** rather than the text. For reading for pleasure, **Nation (2013)** recommends that learners know about **98 % of the words**, which is why **graded readers** are such a valuable form of adapted material. For example, a teacher can use a real recipe for pancakes, keep the original pictures and numbers, but simplify the instructions to short imperatives (*Mix the eggs and the milk*).

## 5. Student collaboration in material design

Finally, pupils themselves can become creators of materials, which is the last part of the title and one of the most motivating practices.

When pupils collaborate in designing materials, they develop **ownership**, **creativity** and **autonomy**, and they use English for a real purpose. Some examples are:
- **Class books** with stories, descriptions or recipes written by the pupils.
- **Flashcards** and **picture dictionaries** drawn by the class.
- **Board games** and **card games** with questions in English.
- **Posters** and **word walls** for the classroom.
- **Digital creations**, such as audio stories, short videos or presentations.
- **Materials for younger pupils**, such as a picture book or a song with actions.

**Example (5th year): *"A board game for the first cycle"***. In groups, fifth-year pupils design a board game to help first-year pupils learn animal words. They choose the animals, draw the cards, write simple instructions with pictures and record the pronunciation of each word. Finally, they visit the first-year class and play the games with them. In this way, older pupils review language, write for a real audience and develop responsibility, while younger pupils enjoy a game created especially for them.

## 6. Attention to diversity and accessibility

Of course, materials must be accessible to every pupil. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, teachers should offer materials in **multiple formats**: printed and digital, with audio, images and subtitles. Pupils with **dyslexia** benefit from clear fonts, adequate spacing and audio versions; pupils with **visual impairments** from enlarged materials or screen readers; and newly arrived pupils from picture dictionaries and visual instructions. In addition, teachers must respect **copyright**, using materials with appropriate licences, such as Creative Commons resources, and they must protect pupils' **privacy** when sharing their creations online.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, materials should support **continuous and formative assessment**, for instance by including self-assessment checklists and tasks that allow teachers to observe pupils' progress.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the train ticket from London. That small piece of paper turned a textbook page into a real journey. As we have seen, good materials follow clear **principles**, textbooks should be **selected critically and used flexibly**, **authentic materials** bring the real world into the classroom when they are used for real purposes, and **adapted materials** make that world accessible. Moreover, when pupils **create their own materials**, learning becomes even more meaningful. Our challenge as teachers is to become **critical selectors, creative adapters and designers** of materials, so that every resource in our classroom brings our pupils one step closer to using English in real life.
