## 1. Introduction

A two-year-old Spanish girl tells her mother *"Yo cabo"* instead of *"yo quepo"*. Some years later, in her English class, the same girl writes *"Yesterday I goed to the park"* and *"He go to school"*. Neither error was ever taught to her: no adult says *cabo* or *goed*. These mistakes show that children are not simply copying what they hear, but **building rules** in their minds. Understanding how this happens, both in the mother tongue and in a foreign language, is the aim of this topic.

This knowledge is essential for teachers. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha establish a **communicative and competence-based** approach to the foreign language, together with continuous, formative assessment. Both choices are rooted in what linguistics and research on language acquisition have taught us over the last century.

To develop this topic, I will first review the main contributions of linguistics to language teaching. Then I will describe how children acquire their first language and how a foreign language is learned. After that, I will compare both processes, considering the age factor, and finally I will draw some implications for the classroom before concluding.

## 2. Contributions of linguistics to foreign language teaching

### 2.1. Structuralism and behaviourism

To begin with, modern linguistics was born with **Saussure**, who described language as a **system of signs** and distinguished between ***langue***, the shared system, and ***parole***, its individual use. In the United States, **Bloomfield (1933)** developed **structuralism**, which described languages as systems of patterns at different levels: sounds, words and sentences. When this view was combined with **behaviourism**, which explained learning as **habit formation** through imitation and reinforcement (**Skinner, 1957**), the result was the **audio-lingual method**, with its dialogues and pattern drills. Its legacy includes the importance of oral language and of practice.

### 2.2. Generativism

However, in his famous review of Skinner, **Chomsky (1959)** argued that behaviourism could not explain how children produce sentences they have never heard. Later, **Chomsky (1965)** proposed that humans have an innate **Language Acquisition Device (LAD)**, and distinguished between **competence**, the speaker's knowledge of the language, and **performance**, its actual use. Although Chomsky did not propose a teaching method, his ideas changed the view of the learner: from someone who imitates to someone who **actively builds rules**, as the *goed* example shows.

### 2.3. Sociolinguistics and functionalism

Nevertheless, Chomsky's ideal speaker ignored the social side of language. **Hymes (1972)** therefore proposed **communicative competence**, which includes knowing what to say, to whom, when and how, and **Halliday (1975)** studied the **functions** children use language for, such as obtaining things, interacting or imagining. These contributions led to the **communicative approach**, which is the basis of our current curriculum.

### 2.4. Applied linguistics and research on second language acquisition

Finally, from the 1970s onwards, **applied linguistics** and research on **second language acquisition (SLA)** began to study how learners actually learn languages, combining linguistics, psychology and education. Many of the ideas in the rest of this topic, such as interlanguage, input and interaction, come from this field.

## 3. First language acquisition

Having seen what linguistics has contributed, let us now turn to the second part of the title, the process of language learning, starting with the mother tongue.

### 3.1. Stages of acquisition

All children, whatever their language, go through similar **stages**. After a pre-linguistic period of crying and **cooing**, they start **babbling** at around six months (*ba-ba-ba*). Around their first birthday, they produce their **first words**, often used as whole sentences (*"Milk!"* meaning *"I want milk"*). Between eighteen months and two years, they combine **two words** (*"Daddy car"*), then move to **telegraphic speech** without function words, and by the age of four or five they master most of the basic grammar of their language.

### 3.2. Theories of acquisition

But how do children achieve this? Several theories have tried to explain it. The **behaviourist** view (**Skinner, 1957**) emphasised imitation and reinforcement, but it cannot explain creative errors such as *cabo*. The **innatist** view (**Chomsky, 1965**) proposed an innate capacity for language. The **cognitive** view, associated with **Piaget**, linked language development to general cognitive development. Finally, the **social-interactionist** view highlighted the role of interaction with adults: **Vygotsky (1978)** described the **zone of proximal development**, the distance between what a child can do alone and with help, and **Bruner (1983)** proposed a **Language Acquisition Support System (LASS)**, the routines and formats, such as reading picture books together, through which adults support children's language. Today, most researchers agree that acquisition results from the **interaction** between children's innate abilities and a rich social environment.

## 4. Foreign language learning

In the same way, research has tried to explain how a second or foreign language is learned.

### 4.1. Krashen's hypotheses

One of the most influential models was proposed by **Krashen (1982)**, who formulated five hypotheses: the **acquisition-learning** distinction; the **monitor**, by which learned knowledge only edits output; the **natural order** of acquisition; the **input hypothesis**, according to which we acquire language by understanding messages slightly above our level; and the **affective filter**, an emotional barrier that blocks input when learners are anxious. These ideas have been criticised because they are difficult to test, but they have strongly influenced teaching, especially the importance of **comprehensible input** and a **positive climate**.

### 4.2. Interlanguage and the role of errors

Equally important was the change in the view of errors. **Corder (1967)** argued that learners' errors are **significant**, because they reveal the learner's developing system. Later, **Selinker (1972)** called this system **interlanguage**: a dynamic system, different from both the mother tongue and the target language, which gradually approaches the target. For example, *"He go"* shows that the pupil has not yet incorporated third-person *-s*, while *"I goed"* shows that they have learned the past *-ed* rule and are overgeneralising it. Both are steps forward, not failures.

### 4.3. Interaction and output

Furthermore, **Long (1996)** showed that **interaction**, in which learners **negotiate meaning** (*"Sorry? What do you mean?"*), adapts input to their needs, and **Swain (1985)** argued that producing **output** makes learners notice what they cannot yet say. Together with input, these ideas form the basis of communicative teaching.

## 5. First language acquisition and foreign language learning compared

With both processes described, we can now compare them, as the title requires.

### 5.1. Similarities and differences

There are clear **similarities**: in both cases, learners need **meaningful input**, go through **developmental stages**, make **creative errors** and learn through **interaction**. However, there are also important **differences**. The mother tongue is acquired with **thousands of hours** of input, in natural contexts and with strong emotional bonds. By contrast, in Spain, a foreign language is learned at school with **a few hours a week**, by learners who already have a first language, which can help through positive **transfer** (*hospital*, *animal*) or interfere (*"I have nine years"*). In addition, school learners have greater **cognitive maturity** and can benefit from explicit explanations.

### 5.2. The age factor

This leads to a frequently debated question: is it always better to start earlier? **Lenneberg (1967)** proposed a **critical period** for language acquisition, ending around puberty, and young children who live immersed in a language usually reach native-like pronunciation. However, school contexts are very different. The **Barcelona Age Factor (BAF) project**, edited by **Muñoz (2006)**, found that, with the same number of school hours, **older starters learned faster** than younger ones, and early starters did not show a clear advantage in the short and medium term. Consequently, the belief that "the earlier, the better" is only true when early exposure is **abundant and of high quality**; with a few hours a week, **time, intensity and quality** matter more than age alone.

## 6. Implications for the classroom

All these contributions lead to some practical implications for teaching English in Primary:
- Provide **rich, comprehensible input**, with English as the main classroom language and stories, songs and routines.
- Create opportunities for **interaction and output**, such as information gaps, games and role-plays.
- See **errors as evidence of learning** and use feedback that helps pupils to notice them.
- Combine communication with **explicit attention to form**, which the meta-analysis by **Norris and Ortega (2000)** showed to be effective.
- Use the mother tongue as a **resource** for transfer and reflection, without replacing English.
- Maintain a **positive affective climate** and maximise **time and intensity** of exposure.

**Example (4th year).** After a story about a boy's weekend, the teacher writes some pupils' sentences on the board, such as *"I goed"* and *"I went"*, and asks the class to discover which is correct and why. Pupils find the pattern of regular and irregular verbs and then use them to tell their own weekend in pairs.

Finally, these processes also explain why pupils progress at different rates. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, teachers should respect each pupil's **interlanguage stage**: a pupil with a **developmental language disorder** needs more input, modelling and repetition; a **newly arrived pupil** may go through a natural silent period; and a pupil with high ability can reflect explicitly on rules. Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**, recording progress over time rather than only counting errors, for example through short recordings of the same task at the beginning and end of the year.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to *"yo cabo"* and *"I goed"*. These errors show that children are **active builders of language**, both in their mother tongue and in English. As we have seen, linguistics has moved from describing structures and habits to understanding language as an **innate, social and communicative** capacity, and research has shown that foreign language learning depends on **input, interaction, output** and **attention to form**, rather than on age alone. Our challenge as teachers is to understand these processes, so that we can see every error not as a failure, but as a **window on our pupils' learning**.
