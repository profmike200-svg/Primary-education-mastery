# Bloques comunes del temario: estudiar una vez, usar en muchos temas

> Objetivo: reducir el esfuerzo de memorización. Los 25 temas comparten **familias** (introducción y conclusión parecidas), **paquetes de bibliografía** y **bloques de contenido** que pueden aprenderse **tal cual** y encajarse en varios temas. Los textos de los bloques están en inglés, con citas narrativas (Autor (año)) y listos para memorizar. Cada bloque deja un hueco **[EXAMPLE]** para adaptar el ejemplo al tema concreto.

## 4. Textos de los bloques (para memorizar tal cual)

### B1 · Curricular framework (introduction)
> This topic is closely connected to the current curriculum. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)** and **Royal Decree 157/2022** establish that the foreign language area must develop pupils' **communicative competence**, and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha organises it into six specific competences: comprehension, production, interaction, mediation, plurilingualism and interculturality. These competences contribute directly to the key competences in linguistic communication and plurilingualism, and they are developed through learning situations that are meaningful for pupils. **[EXAMPLE: one sentence linking the topic to one specific competence.]**

### B2 · Attention to diversity
> Every class is diverse, and our planning must reach every pupil from the very beginning. **Universal Design for Learning**, as described by **CAST (2018)**, proposes offering multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression, and this principle is consistent with **Decree 85/2018**, which regulates inclusive education in Castilla-La Mancha. In practice, this means combining visual, oral and written support, allowing different ways of showing learning and adjusting the level of challenge. For instance, a pupil with dyslexia can show their understanding orally, a newly arrived pupil can rely on pictures and a buddy, and pupils with a high level can take on extension tasks. **[EXAMPLE: one adaptation specific to the topic.]**

### B3 · Assessment
> Assessment closes the circle of teaching and learning. According to **Order 185/2022**, assessment in Primary Education must be **continuous, formative and global**, and it must be based on the assessment criteria of each cycle. Therefore, instead of relying only on tests, we should use a variety of instruments, such as observation scales, rubrics shared with pupils in advance, short performance tasks and *can-do* self-assessment checklists. In this way, assessment not only measures learning but also helps pupils to improve. **[EXAMPLE: the instrument that best fits the topic.]**

### B4 · Communicative competence
> The concept of communicative competence was introduced by **Hymes (1972)**, who argued that knowing a language means knowing not only its grammar, but also what to say, to whom, when and how. Later, **Canale and Swain (1980)** described three components: grammatical, sociolinguistic and strategic competence, and **Canale (1983)** added discourse competence. **Bachman (1990)** reorganised these ideas into organisational and pragmatic competence. Today, the **Council of Europe (2020)** describes communication in terms of reception, production, interaction and mediation, and this is the model behind the specific competences of our curriculum. For example, when a pupil says *"Can you repeat, please?"* because they have not understood, they are using strategic competence.

### B5 · Input, interaction and output
> Research on second language acquisition offers three key ideas for the classroom. First, **Krashen (1982)** highlighted the importance of **comprehensible input**, that is, messages that pupils can understand thanks to context and support. Second, **Long (1996)** showed that interaction, in which learners negotiate meaning, adapts input to their needs. Third, **Swain (1985)** argued that producing language, or output, makes learners notice what they cannot yet say. Together, these ideas suggest a simple rule: pupils need to hear a lot of meaningful English, to interact in it and to use it themselves. For example, in an information-gap activity, pupils listen, ask and answer to complete a task.

### B6 · Listening stages
> A listening activity is more effective when it is organised in three stages. In the **pre-listening** stage, the teacher activates prior knowledge, pre-teaches a few key words and gives pupils a clear purpose. In the **while-listening** stage, pupils complete a task that requires little production, such as pointing, numbering or responding physically. Finally, in the **post-listening** stage, they use the language in a new way, for instance by retelling or acting out the text. As **Cameron (2001)** explains, young learners rely heavily on context and visual support, so pictures and gestures are essential at every stage. **[EXAMPLE]**

### B7 · Speaking progression and feedback
> Oral production develops gradually: pupils first **imitate** songs and chants, then practise structures in **controlled** activities, then produce language with support in **guided** tasks, and finally reach **free production**. **Brumfit (1984)** reminds us that both accuracy and fluency matter, so fluency activities should not be constantly interrupted. When correction is needed, the meta-analysis by **Lyster and Saito (2010)** shows that oral feedback is effective, especially with younger learners, and that prompts which lead pupils to correct themselves work better than simple recasts. For example, the teacher can repeat an error with rising intonation, *"Yesterday I… go?"*, so that the pupil finds the correct form.

### B8 · Reading and writing progression
> Written language should come after pupils are familiar with words orally (Cameron, 2001). Because English spelling is not transparent, pupils also need explicit teaching of the most common sound-spelling patterns. Reading moves from recognising known words to understanding short texts, and **Nation (2013)** points out that reading for pleasure requires knowing around 98 % of the words in a text. Writing, in turn, moves from copying, to guided writing with models and frames, and finally to free writing; teaching the writing process explicitly improves children's texts, as the meta-analysis by **Graham et al. (2012)** shows. **[EXAMPLE]**

### B9 · Storytelling
> Stories are one of the most powerful resources in the Primary English classroom. They offer meaningful, repeated input, they connect with children's emotions and imagination, and their predictable structures allow early production. **Ellis and Brewster (2014)** propose working with stories in three stages, before, during and after the telling, and **Wright (2009)** shows how stories can become the starting point for speaking, drama and writing activities. For example, after listening to a story with a repeated sentence, pupils can join in, act it out and finally create a new page for a class book.

### B10 · Games and creativity
> Games turn practice into play. They provide a real reason to use the language, they allow a great deal of repetition without boredom and they lower anxiety. **Wright, Betteridge and Buckby (2006)** classify language games according to their aim, such as games for vocabulary, structures or communication, and they stress that a good game must have a clear linguistic objective. Moreover, games can be combined with **retrieval practice**: a quick memory game at the beginning of a lesson helps pupils to remember the words from previous units. **[EXAMPLE]**

### B11 · Cooperative group work
> Group work becomes truly cooperative only when certain conditions are met. **Johnson and Johnson (2009)** identify five essential elements: **positive interdependence**, **individual accountability**, promotive interaction, social skills and group processing. In the English classroom, this means giving each pupil a clear role, such as reader, writer or timekeeper, and making sure that everyone must contribute to the final product. For example, in a jigsaw activity, each member reads a different part of a text and explains it to the group, which also develops mediation.

### B12 · Technology and artificial intelligence
> Technology can enrich language learning, but only when it is used with a clear pedagogical purpose. The **OECD (2015)** found that countries which invested heavily in computers did not automatically improve their results, and that moderate, well-planned use worked better than intensive use. Frameworks such as TPACK, proposed by **Mishra and Koehler (2006)**, remind us that technology must be combined with pedagogical and content knowledge. Today, digital tools and artificial intelligence can offer authentic input, pronunciation practice and immediate feedback, always with critical and safe use. **[EXAMPLE]**

### B13 · Culture and interculturality
> Language and culture cannot be separated. **Byram (1997)** describes **intercultural communicative competence** as the ability to communicate with people from other cultures while respecting their perspectives, and the **Council of Europe (2020)** includes plurilingual and pluricultural competence among its descriptors. In the Primary classroom, this means discovering festivals, stories, games and daily life in English-speaking countries, going beyond the United Kingdom and the United States, and avoiding stereotypes. For instance, pupils can compare a typical school day in Spain and in India through a video call.

### B14 · Motivation and affective factors
> Motivation is one of the strongest predictors of success in language learning. **Gardner (1985)** distinguished between **integrative motivation**, the wish to connect with speakers of the language, and **instrumental motivation**, the wish to obtain a practical benefit, while **Ryan and Deci (2000)** showed that **intrinsic motivation** grows when learners feel autonomous, competent and connected to others. **Dörnyei (2001)** proposed practical motivational strategies, such as creating a pleasant atmosphere and setting achievable goals. In Primary, this translates into meaningful tasks, small successes and a classroom where mistakes are part of learning.

### B15 · Conclusion template
> **[Return to the hook in one sentence.]** As we have seen throughout this topic, **[summary of the two or three key ideas]**. These ideas are not only theoretical: they shape the way we plan, teach and assess every day in the English classroom. Our challenge as teachers is to **[topic-specific challenge]**, so that every child, whatever their starting point, can enjoy learning English and use it to communicate with others.
