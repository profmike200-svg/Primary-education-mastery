# Topic 22

## 1. Introduction

Two teachers prepare the same English lesson: a speaking game about pets followed by a short song. In the first classroom, pupils sit in rows, nobody knows who to work with, the game starts late and the song is cut short when the bell rings. In the second classroom, pupils move quickly into pairs they already know, the furniture is arranged in advance, the teacher uses a short routine to start and a clear signal to change activity, and the lesson ends calmly with the song. The activities are identical, but the **learning is very different**. The difference lies in the organisation of the class, the subject of this topic.

Indeed, organisation is often invisible, but it is a basic condition for learning. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha promote **active methodologies**, **cooperative work** and **inclusive** classrooms, and these can only work if grouping, space and time are carefully planned. Moreover, **Order 121/2022** gives schools autonomy to organise their resources within their **educational project**.

To develop this topic, I will analyse the variables mentioned in the title: student grouping, the distribution of space and time, and the selection of methodology according to pupils' characteristics. Finally, I will describe the teacher's role, before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Student grouping according to the purpose of the activity

To begin with, there is no single best way of grouping pupils: the right grouping depends on the **purpose** of the activity.
- **Whole class**: useful for presenting new language, storytelling, songs, routines and giving instructions. It builds a sense of community, but it offers few opportunities for each pupil to speak.
- **Pairs**: ideal for speaking practice, information gaps and peer checking. They are easy to organise and multiply speaking time; for example, in a pet survey, every pupil speaks at the same time.
- **Small groups** of three to five: appropriate for projects, games, drama and creative tasks.
- **Individual work**: necessary for reflection, reading, writing and self-assessment.

Furthermore, grouping should be **flexible**, changing according to the activity and the pupils' needs. **Heterogeneous** groups allow pupils to help each other, while temporary **homogeneous** groups can be useful for specific support or extension. Forming groups quickly is also a skill: pupils can be paired with picture cards (*find the person with the same animal*), with named sticks drawn at random or with stable "talk partners" for the whole term, which saves time and avoids pupils always choosing the same friends.

However, putting pupils together does not guarantee that they cooperate. **Johnson and Johnson (2009)** identified five essential elements of **cooperative learning**: **positive interdependence**, **individual accountability**, **promotive interaction**, **social skills** and **group processing**. In practice, this means giving each pupil a **role**, such as reader, writer, timekeeper or reporter, and making sure everyone must contribute. For example, in a group poster about animals, each pupil is responsible for one animal and must present it.

## 3. Space distribution

Having decided how pupils will work together, the next variable is the **classroom space**, which should support those groupings.

**Harmer (2015)** describes several seating arrangements, each with advantages and limitations. **Orderly rows** allow the teacher to see everyone and work well for presentations, but they make interaction difficult. A **horseshoe** or **circle** encourages whole-class discussion and eye contact. **Tables in groups** favour cooperative work, although they may make whole-class attention harder. Consequently, the ideal is a **flexible space** in which furniture can be moved quickly, for example clearing a space in the middle of the room for drama, TPR or movement games.

In addition, the classroom should be an **English environment**. Some useful elements are:
- An **English corner** with picture books, graded readers, games and a listening station.
- A **word wall** with key vocabulary and **speaking frames** (*Can I…? I think… because…*).
- **Displays** of pupils' work, which increase motivation and recycle language.
- **Visual routines**, such as a weather chart, a calendar and a feelings board.

Whether English is taught in a specific language classroom or in the pupils' ordinary classroom, these elements help to create an atmosphere in which English is visible and present. For instance, a portable "English box" with flashcards, puppets and the weather chart can turn any classroom into an English space in seconds. Finally, learning can also take place **outside the classroom**: the playground for active games, the school library for reading time, the garden for a nature walk describing plants, or a **digital space**, such as a class blog or an eTwinning platform, where pupils share their work with others.

## 4. Time distribution

Space must be combined with time, the third variable in the title.

At the level of the **session**, a clear structure helps pupils to feel secure and makes the most of time. A typical English lesson includes a **warm-up** with routines (greeting, date, weather, a song); **main activities**, in which new language is presented, practised and used; and a **closing** activity to review what has been learned, for example with an exit ticket or a short game. Over the **unit** and the **year**, time is organised through learning situations distributed across the three terms (see topic 21).

**Routines** deserve special attention. According to the guidance report of the **Education Endowment Foundation (2019)** on improving behaviour, consistent **routines** and explicit teaching of learning behaviours are among the most effective ways of creating a positive classroom climate. In English, routines are doubly useful, because they also provide repeated, meaningful language.

Moreover, the **pace** and **variety** of activities must match pupils' age. Younger children need short activities and frequent changes, alternating **stirring** activities, which energise the class, such as action songs, with **settling** activities, which calm it, such as colouring while listening. For example, after a lively game of *Simon says*, a short drawing dictation helps first-year pupils to calm down before the next task.

Transitions between activities are another critical moment, because a great deal of time can be lost. Clear **instructions** help: the teacher should give them in short steps, **demonstrate** the activity with a pupil, and check understanding with **instruction-checking questions**, such as *"Do you work alone or in pairs?"* or *"Do you write or draw?"*, before saying *"Go!"*.

## 5. Selection of methodology according to pupils' characteristics

However, the organisation of the class also depends on **who** our pupils are, which leads us to the fourth variable.

Firstly, methodology must be adapted to **age and cognitive development**. **Cameron (2001)** and **Pinter (2017)** explain that younger children learn through meaning, movement, routines and concrete materials, and they have limited attention and literacy in English, so oral work, songs, stories and TPR are essential. Older pupils, on the other hand, can reflect on language, work more autonomously, read and write longer texts and carry out projects.

Secondly, teachers must consider pupils' **level of English**, their **interests**, their **motivation** and the **context**, such as whether the school has a bilingual programme. For example, a sixth-year group that loves football can carry out a project about a famous English-speaking football club. Similarly, the same topic can be approached very differently depending on age: in the first cycle, "animals" may mean a song, masks and a TPR game, while in the third cycle it may become a research project about endangered species, with reading, note-taking and an oral presentation.

Thirdly, methodology must respond to **diversity**. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, teachers should plan multiple means of engagement, representation and expression. Pupils with **ADHD** may sit near the teacher and benefit from movement breaks, pupils with **autism spectrum disorder** from visual timetables and advance notice of changes, and newly arrived pupils from a buddy and visual support.

## 6. The teacher's role

Finally, all these variables depend on the teacher, whose role is the last element of the title. **Harmer (2015)** describes several roles that the teacher adopts according to the activity: **controller** when presenting or giving instructions; **organiser** when setting up groups and activities; **prompter** when helping pupils who are stuck; **participant** when joining a game or role-play; **resource** when providing language; and **assessor** when observing and giving feedback.

In addition, effective classroom management requires what **Kounin (1970)** called **withitness**: the teacher's awareness of everything that is happening in the classroom, which allows them to prevent problems before they grow. Other key aspects are the use of **English as the main language** of the classroom, with clear **classroom language** for instructions; clear **signals** for attention and transitions, such as a song or a hand signal; and a **positive, respectful climate** in which mistakes are part of learning. Above all, the teacher is the main **model** of English: by speaking clearly, using gestures, praising effort and showing enthusiasm for the language, the teacher shows pupils what a confident English user looks like.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the two classrooms that taught the same lesson with very different results. As we have seen, learning depends not only on what we teach, but also on **how we organise** grouping, space and time, and on how we adapt methodology to our pupils' **age, level, interests and needs**. The teacher, moving between different **roles** and keeping a constant awareness of the class, is the key to making all these variables work together. Our challenge is to make organisation **invisible**, so that what pupils notice is not the structure, but the pleasure of learning English.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Grouping by purpose: whole class, pairs, small groups, individual; flexible; cooperative learning with 5 elements (**Johnson & Johnson, 2009**).
> - Space: rows, horseshoe, groups (**Harmer, 2015**); English corner, word wall, displays, visual routines.
> - Time: warm-up → main activities → closing; routines (**EEF, 2019**); stirring and settling activities.
> - Methodology by age and development (**Cameron, 2001**; **Pinter, 2017**), level, interests and diversity (**CAST, 2018**; Decree 85/2018).
> - Teacher's roles (**Harmer, 2015**); withitness (**Kounin, 1970**); English as classroom language; positive climate.
