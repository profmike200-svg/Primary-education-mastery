## 1. Introduction

During a shop role-play, a fifth-year pupil confidently said: *"I want to buy a **sheet**, please"*, while pointing at a toy **sheep**. The whole class laughed. Only one sound had changed, the final /p/ of *sheep* had become a /t/, but it was enough to change the meaning of the message. This small anecdote shows that **pronunciation is not a decoration of language**: it is what allows us to be understood.

For this reason, pronunciation deserves a central place in the Primary English classroom. **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha include sound patterns, **rhythm, stress and intonation** within the basic knowledge of the Communication block, linked to the specific competences of comprehension, production and interaction. Moreover, research supports this place: the meta-analysis by **Lee, Jang and Plonsky (2015)** found that **explicit pronunciation instruction is effective**, and **Derwing and Munro (2005)** showed that the realistic goal is **intelligibility**, not a native accent.

With this in mind, I will first explain some key concepts and describe the English phonological system. Then I will deal with perception, discrimination, rhythm and stress, followed by the models and techniques of learning. Finally, I will analyse phonetic correction, diversity and assessment, before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Phonetics and phonology: key concepts

Before describing the system, it is useful to clarify some basic terms. **Phonetics** studies how speech sounds are physically produced and perceived, whereas **phonology** studies how sounds are organised in a particular language and how they **distinguish meaning**.

The central unit of phonology is the **phoneme**, the smallest unit of sound that changes meaning. We can identify phonemes through **minimal pairs**, words that differ in only one sound, such as *ship* /ʃɪp/ and *sheep* /ʃiːp/. A phoneme may have several **allophones**, slightly different realisations that do not change meaning; for example, the /p/ in *pin* is aspirated, with a small puff of air, but the /p/ in *spin* is not. Finally, we distinguish **segmental features**, the individual sounds, from **suprasegmental features**, such as stress, rhythm and intonation, which affect longer stretches of speech.

## 3. Description of the English phonological system

Having defined these concepts, let us now describe the system itself, taking standard British English, or **Received Pronunciation (RP)**, as the reference model described by **Roach (2009)**.

### 3.1. Segmental features

According to the usual description, RP has **44 phonemes**: **20 vowels** and **24 consonants**. The vowels include **12 monophthongs**, which may be short, such as /ɪ/ in *sit*, or long, such as /iː/ in *seat*, and **8 diphthongs**, in which the tongue glides from one position to another, as in *boy* /ɔɪ/ or *go* /əʊ/. Vowels are classified according to **tongue height**, **tongue position** (front, central or back) and **lip rounding**. Consonants, in turn, are classified by **place of articulation** (for example, bilabial /p/, /b/, /m/), **manner of articulation** (plosive, fricative, nasal…) and **voicing**: /s/ is voiceless, while /z/ is voiced.

### 3.2. Suprasegmental features

However, sounds alone do not make English intelligible; suprasegmental features are equally important.
- **Word stress**: one syllable is stronger than the others, and its position can change: *PHOtograph*, *phoTOgrapher*, *photoGRAphic*.
- **Sentence stress**: content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives) are usually stressed, while function words are not: *I WANT to GO to the PARK*.
- **Rhythm**: English has traditionally been described as **stress-timed**, with stressed syllables at fairly regular intervals, and Spanish as **syllable-timed**. Although research has questioned this strict division, it remains a useful teaching idea.
- **Weak forms and the schwa** /ə/: unstressed words are reduced, so *to* in *go to school* sounds like /tə/. The schwa is the **most frequent vowel** in English.
- **Connected speech**: words are linked (*an apple* sounds like *a-napple*) and some sounds disappear (*nex(t) day*).
- **Intonation**: a **falling tone** usually signals statements and *wh-* questions, whereas a **rising tone** is common in *yes/no* questions: *Are you ready?* ↗.

### 3.3. A contrastive analysis with Spanish

Since our pupils are Spanish speakers, a **contrastive analysis** helps us to anticipate their difficulties. Spanish has **5 vowels**, while English has 12 monophthongs, so pupils tend to confuse **vowel length and quality** (*ship/sheep*, *full/fool*) and to replace the schwa with full vowels. Furthermore, standard Spanish lacks phonemes such as /v/, /z/ and /ʃ/, so *very* may sound like *berry*. Pupils also tend to add a vowel before initial **s + consonant** (*"eschool"*), to drop **final consonants and clusters**, especially in *-ed* and *-s* endings, to pronounce English /h/ as the stronger Spanish *jota*, and to give every syllable the same weight, which makes their English sound flat.

## 4. Perception, discrimination, rhythms and stresses

Knowing these difficulties, the next question is how to address them, and the answer begins with the ear.

### 4.1. Perception and discrimination

**Perception comes before production**: children cannot produce a contrast they cannot hear. Therefore, the first step is **discrimination** activities, in which pupils show that they hear the difference without having to produce it. For example, in a **minimal-pair game**, pupils stand up when they hear *sheep* and sit down when they hear *ship*, or hold up the right picture. Other useful activities are minimal-pair bingo, *odd one out* (*cat, hat, cake*) and sorting words into "sound boxes".

### 4.2. Rhythm and stress

Similarly, rhythm and stress are learned best through the body and through music. Pupils can **clap the syllables** and stomp on the stressed one (*ba-NA-na*), and **chants** are an excellent resource: **Graham (1978)**, in her *Jazz Chants*, showed how the natural rhythm of spoken English can be practised through short, rhythmic texts. Songs and nursery rhymes work in the same way, and **backchaining**, repeating a long sentence from the end (*park → to the park → go to the park*), helps pupils keep the rhythm.

**Example (5th year): *-ed detectives*.** Pupils listen to past forms and sort them into three boxes: /t/ (*worked*), /d/ (*played*) and /ɪd/ (*wanted*). Then, in pairs, they try to discover the rule: /ɪd/ appears after /t/ or /d/. Finally, they tell a short story about their weekend, paying attention to the endings.

## 5. Models and techniques of learning

Once we know what to teach, we need to decide how to teach it and which model to follow.

### 5.1. Approaches to teaching pronunciation

**Celce-Murcia, Brinton and Goodwin (2010)** describe two broad approaches. The **intuitive-imitative approach** relies on listening to models and imitating them, which suits young children, who are excellent imitators. The **analytic-linguistic approach**, on the other hand, offers explicit information, such as articulation diagrams, phonemic symbols or rules, and is more useful from the third cycle. In practice, an **integrative approach** combines both within communicative tasks; for example, pupils imitate a chant and then discover why some words are said more quickly.

### 5.2. Which model of English?

In addition, teachers must choose a **reference model**, usually British or American English, while also exposing pupils to other accents. Since English is now a global language, **Jenkins (2000)** proposed the **Lingua Franca Core**, a set of features that matter most for international intelligibility, such as most consonant sounds, vowel length and nuclear stress. Consequently, the priority is not to sound native, but to be **understood by speakers from anywhere**.

### 5.3. Techniques for each cycle

Finally, techniques should match pupils' age. In the **first cycle**, songs, rhymes, Total Physical Response and imitation games dominate. In the **second cycle**, minimal pairs, chants and **phonics** links between sounds and spelling are added (see topic 10). In the **third cycle**, pupils can learn a few **phonemic symbols** for difficult sounds, mark stress in new words and **record themselves** to compare their pronunciation with a model, including through digital tools used critically.

## 6. The phonetic correction

Even with good teaching, errors will appear, which leads us to the last part of the title: phonetic correction.

First, correction needs **priorities**. Following **Derwing and Munro (2005)**, teachers should focus on errors that affect **intelligibility**, such as vowel length, word stress and final consonants, rather than on every feature of a foreign accent. For instance, *"I live in a big HOUSE"* with a Spanish accent is perfectly clear, whereas confusing *live* and *leave* can cause misunderstandings.

Second, correction techniques should encourage pupils to notice and repair their own errors. The meta-analysis by **Lyster and Saito (2010)** found that **prompts** are more effective than **recasts** and that young learners benefit particularly from feedback. In practice, the teacher can use **gestures**, such as stretching their hands apart for a long vowel; **mirrors**, to observe lip rounding; **minimal-pair contrasts**; and short **recordings**.

Third, the emotional climate is essential. Correction must never cause embarrassment: it can be done chorally, individually and discreetly, or through games, so that pupils see it as **help, not judgement**.

## 7. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, all these activities must be accessible to every pupil. In line with **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, pupils with **hearing loss** need a good seating position, visual cues of articulation and captions; pupils with a **developmental language disorder** benefit from extra modelling and repetition; and pupils with **dyslexia** gain from oral priority and **phonological awareness** work.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment must be **continuous and formative**. An **observation scale** focused on intelligibility, stress and key sounds, together with **recordings** at the beginning and end of the year, allows pupils to hear their own progress and supports **self-assessment**.

## 8. Conclusion

To conclude, let us go back to the "sheet" that should have been a "sheep". Our pupils do not need to sound like native speakers, but they **do need to be understood**. A good pronunciation programme starts with **listening**, moves through **discrimination** and **playful practice** of rhythm and stress, and treats **correction as help**. If we achieve this, the next time our pupil goes shopping in English, the only thing the class will laugh at is a good joke.
