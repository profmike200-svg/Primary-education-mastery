## 1. Introduction

When a family plans a trip, they first decide **where they want to go**, then they choose the **route**, the **stops** and the **time** for each part, and they check along the way whether they are on the right track. Nobody would buy train tickets before choosing the destination. Programming in teaching works in exactly the same way: we first decide what pupils should achieve, then we plan how to get there and how to check their progress. Good programming is the map that guides a whole school year, and it is the subject of this topic.

Indeed, programming is a central professional skill. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha establish that teachers must plan teaching through **learning situations** that develop the **specific competences** of each area, while **Order 185/2022** requires **continuous and formative assessment**. Moreover, in the oppositions, candidates must present and defend a didactic programme, which shows its importance in the profession.

To develop this topic, I will first explain the concept and principles of programming. Then I will describe programming units, which are now called learning situations, and the criteria for sequencing and timing objectives and contents. After that, I will analyse the selection of methodology for learning and assessment activities, and I will refer to diversity before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Programming in the foreign language area

### 2.1. Concept and levels

To begin with, **programming** is the process of planning teaching for a specific group of pupils, adapting the official curriculum to their context. It takes place at several levels: the **didactic programme** of the area for each year or cycle, which is agreed by the teaching team; the **classroom programming**, in which each teacher organises the year; and the **learning situations**, which are the units of work.

### 2.2. Principles

A good programme should follow several principles. It must be **coherent** with the curriculum and with the school's educational project; **realistic**, adapted to the time and resources available; **flexible**, so that it can be adjusted when needed; and **inclusive**, so that every pupil can progress. For example, a programme that plans a long written project in the same week as the school play is not realistic, and one that cannot change when a group needs more time on a difficult structure is not flexible.

### 2.3. Backward design

In addition, a very useful model is **backward design**, proposed by **Wiggins and McTighe (2005)**. It consists of three stages: first, **identify the desired results**, that is, what pupils should understand and be able to do; second, **determine acceptable evidence**, deciding how we will know they have achieved it; and third, **plan learning experiences** that lead to those results. For example, if pupils must be able to describe their town to a visitor, the final task could be a video guide, and every activity in the unit should prepare them for it.

## 3. Programming units: learning situations

Having seen the general principles, let us now look at the programming units, the first part of the title.

### 3.1. Elements of a learning situation

Under the current curriculum, programming units are designed as **learning situations**: sets of contextualised activities that lead to a meaningful **final product**. **Decree 81/2022** includes a model in its Annex III, and a learning situation usually contains:
- A **title** and a **context** connected with pupils' lives.
- The **curricular elements**: specific competences, assessment criteria, basic knowledge and key competence descriptors.
- A **sequence of activities**, usually in three phases: **activation**, to awaken interest and prior knowledge; **development**, to learn and practise; and **application**, in which pupils create the final product.
- **Methodology**, **grouping**, **resources** and **timing**.
- **Measures of inclusion**.
- **Assessment**: criteria, instruments and moments.

**Example (4th year): *"Healthy Food Fair"***. In the **activation** phase, pupils watch a short video about breakfasts around the world and say what they had for breakfast. In the **development** phase, they learn food vocabulary, practise *"Do you like…?"* and *"There is / There are"*, read labels and conduct a class survey. In the **application** phase, groups design a healthy menu and present it at a food fair for families. The unit lasts about six sessions and is assessed with an observation scale, a rubric and self-assessment.

## 4. Criteria for sequencing and timing objectives and contents

Once the units are designed, the next step is to put them in order and distribute them in time, the second part of the title.

### 4.1. Sequencing

Several criteria guide **sequencing**. **Bruner (1960)** proposed the **spiral curriculum**, in which key ideas are revisited repeatedly at increasing levels of complexity. In English, this means that topics such as food, family or the weather reappear throughout Primary, each time with richer language: *"I like apples"* in the first cycle becomes *"I prefer fruit because it's healthier"* in the third. Other criteria include moving from the **simple to the complex**, from the **known to the new**, from **oral to written** and from **receptive to productive** skills.

Furthermore, sequencing must include **recycling**. The review by **Dunlosky et al. (2013)** found that **spaced practice** and **retrieval practice** are among the most effective learning techniques, so each unit should include short reviews of language from previous units.

### 4.2. Timing

**Timing** is organised at different scales. Over the **school year**, units are distributed across the three terms, taking into account festivals such as Halloween, Christmas or Easter, which provide cultural content. For instance, a fourth-year programme might include around nine learning situations, three per term, with *"Halloween at our school"* in the first term, *"Healthy Food Fair"* in the second and *"Our town guide"* in the third, leaving some sessions free for review and unexpected needs. At the level of the **unit**, each learning situation usually takes between two and four weeks. Finally, at the level of the **session**, a typical English lesson includes a **warm-up routine**, the **main activities** and a **closing** activity, with shorter and more varied activities for younger pupils.

## 5. Selection of methodology for learning activities

With the map and the timetable ready, we must decide how pupils will learn, the third part of the title.

The methodology should combine **communicative tasks** with **explicit attention to form**, since the meta-analysis by **Norris and Ortega (2000)** showed that explicit instruction is more effective than purely implicit exposure. In addition, **Rosenshine (2012)** summarised principles of effective instruction, such as reviewing previous learning, presenting new material in small steps, providing models, guiding practice and checking understanding frequently. With young learners, **Cameron (2001)** emphasises meaning, routines, visual support and scaffolding.

Consequently, a balanced programme includes different **types of activities**: **introductory** activities to activate knowledge, **development** activities to present and practise language, **consolidation** activities to review, **extension** activities for faster learners, **reinforcement** activities for pupils who need more support, and **final tasks** in which pupils apply what they have learned. In the *"Healthy Food Fair"*, for example, a food guessing game activates prior knowledge, a song and a survey develop the language, a quick memory quiz consolidates it, pupils who finish early write extra menu items, others work with picture cards, and the food fair itself is the final task.

## 6. Selection of methodology for assessment activities

However, planning learning is not enough: we also need to know whether learning is happening, which takes us to assessment.

According to **Order 185/2022**, assessment must be **continuous, formative and global**. In their influential review, **Black and Wiliam (1998)** showed that **formative assessment**, in which information about learning is used to adjust teaching, can significantly raise achievement. Furthermore, **Hattie and Timperley (2007)** explained that effective **feedback** answers three questions: **Where am I going?** (the goals), **How am I going?** (progress), and **Where to next?** (the next steps).

In practice, assessment activities should be **integrated** into learning and use **varied instruments**: **observation scales** for oral interaction, **rubrics** shared with pupils in advance, short **performance tasks**, **portfolios**, **exit tickets** and ***can-do* self-assessment**. For example, at the end of a session, pupils can draw a smiley face next to statements such as *"I can ask about food"*. In addition, each assessment criterion should be observed **several times** and with **different instruments** before reaching a conclusion, and results must be communicated clearly to pupils and families. Finally, teachers should also **assess their own teaching practice**, reflecting on what worked and what should be changed, for example through a short checklist at the end of each unit and pupils' opinions about the activities.

## 7. Attention to diversity

Of course, programming must consider every pupil from the beginning. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, each learning situation should offer **multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression**. For example, instructions can be given orally, visually and with a model; pupils with **dyslexia** can present orally; **newly arrived pupils** can rely on visual support and a buddy; and pupils with **high ability** can take on extension tasks, such as preparing a quiz for the class.

## 8. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the family planning their trip. A good programme, like a good travel plan, starts from the **destination**, organises the **route** and the **time**, and checks **progress** along the way. As we have seen, programming in English is organised through **learning situations** designed with **backward design**, sequenced in a **spiral** with constant recycling, and supported by a methodology that combines communication, explicit teaching and **formative assessment**. Our challenge as teachers is to use the programme as a **map, not a cage**: a guide that gives direction, but that we can adapt whenever our pupils need a different path.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Levels: didactic programme → classroom programming → learning situations; coherent, realistic, flexible, inclusive.
> - Backward design: results → evidence → learning experiences (**Wiggins & McTighe, 2005**).
> - Learning situation (Decree 81/2022, Annex III): context, curricular elements, activation → development → application, methodology, resources, inclusion, assessment.
> - Sequencing: spiral curriculum (**Bruner, 1960**), simple → complex, oral → written, receptive → productive; spaced recycling (**Dunlosky et al., 2013**).
> - Learning methodology: communication + focus on form (**Norris & Ortega, 2000**), principles of instruction (**Rosenshine, 2012**), young learners (**Cameron, 2001**).
> - Assessment: formative (**Black & Wiliam, 1998**); feedback: where am I going, how, where next (**Hattie & Timperley, 2007**); varied instruments.
