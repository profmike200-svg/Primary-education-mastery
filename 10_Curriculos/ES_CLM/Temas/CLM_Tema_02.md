## 1. Introduction

It is the first day of school in a first-year class. The English teacher walks in, smiles, waves and says *"Hello, everyone!"*. Then she points at the chairs, makes a calm downward gesture with both hands and says *"Sit down, please"*. Although none of the children has ever heard that sentence, **everyone sits down**. In that instant, communication has worked, not because of the words alone, but because of the combination of **words, gestures, tone and context**. This topic deals precisely with how communication happens in the foreign language classroom, both verbally and non-verbally.

The importance of this topic is clear. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha establish that the foreign language area must develop **communicative competence**, and they include among the basic knowledge the use of **non-verbal resources** and **communication strategies** to understand and express messages. In Primary, where pupils' English is still limited, these resources are not a complement, but a **basic condition** for communication to take place.

To develop this topic, I will first describe the classroom as a communicative context. Then I will analyse verbal communication and non-verbal communication. After that, I will explain extralinguistic strategies and how we react non-verbally to messages in different contexts. Finally, I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Communication in the foreign language classroom

### 2.1. The classroom as a communicative context

To begin with, we must recognise that, for most of our pupils, the English classroom is **the main, and sometimes the only, place where they use English**. Therefore, it must become a rich communicative context. Research on second language acquisition offers three key ideas. First, **Krashen (1982)** highlighted the importance of **comprehensible input**, that is, messages that pupils can understand thanks to context and support. Second, **Long (1996)** showed that **interaction**, in which learners **negotiate meaning**, adapts input to their needs. Third, **Swain (1985)** argued that producing language, or **output**, makes learners notice what they cannot yet say. In short, pupils need to **hear** a lot of meaningful English, to **interact** in it and to **use** it themselves.

### 2.2. The communicative approach

These ideas are the basis of the **communicative approach**, which is developed in topics 13 and 14. In this approach, the classroom is not a place where pupils learn *about* English, but where they **communicate in English** for real purposes. For example, asking for the scissors in English, playing a guessing game or greeting a visitor are genuine acts of communication, even with very little language.

## 3. Verbal communication in the English classroom

Within this context, verbal communication, the use of words, plays the central role, and its main source is the teacher.

### 3.1. Classroom language and teacher talk

As **Cameron (2001)** explains, the **teacher's talk** is often the richest source of input that young learners receive. Hence, English should be the **main language of the classroom**, used for routines, instructions, praise and classroom management. **Classroom language** includes expressions for the teacher (*Line up, please*, *Well done!*, *Work in pairs*) and for pupils (*Can I go to the toilet, please?*, *How do you say… in English?*, *I don't understand*). When these chunks are used every day, pupils learn them naturally and begin to use English spontaneously.

However, teacher talk must be **adapted**: shorter sentences, a slower pace, clear pronunciation, frequent repetition and, above all, the support of gestures and visuals. The mother tongue can still be used **strategically**, for instance to solve an emotional conflict or to check a complex instruction, but it should not replace English as the normal channel of communication.

### 3.2. Principles for effective verbal communication

In addition, several principles help to make verbal communication effective. Firstly, a **low-anxiety environment**: Krashen (1982) described an **affective filter**, a kind of emotional barrier that blocks input when learners feel anxious. Although this hypothesis is difficult to test, it is widely agreed that children speak more when they feel safe. Secondly, **integrated skills**, because real communication combines listening, speaking, reading and writing. Thirdly, **real purposes**: for example, writing to a partner school through **eTwinning** gives pupils a genuine reason to communicate.

## 4. Non-verbal communication

Words, however, are only part of the message. The second part of the title draws our attention to non-verbal communication.

### 4.1. Concept and classification

**Non-verbal communication** includes all the messages we send without words. Following **Knapp, Hall and Horgan (2014)**, we can distinguish several areas:
- **Kinesics**: body movements, gestures, facial expression, posture and eye contact. For example, a thumbs-up means *"well done"*.
- **Proxemics**: the use of space and distance between people, studied by **Hall (1966)**. For instance, a teacher who crouches down to a child's eye level creates closeness and trust.
- **Paralanguage**: vocal features that accompany words, such as tone, volume, speed and pauses. A whispered *"Let's be quiet now"* can calm a noisy class better than a shout.
- **Physical appearance and the environment**: clothing, the classroom layout and displays also communicate messages.

### 4.2. A note of caution: Mehrabian's percentages

At this point, it is important to avoid a common myth. It is often said that **only 7 % of communication is verbal**, while 38 % depends on the voice and 55 % on the body. These figures come from experiments by **Mehrabian (1971)**, but they referred only to situations in which people communicated **feelings and attitudes** and when words and tone contradicted each other. Mehrabian himself warned that they **cannot be applied to all communication**. Therefore, the correct conclusion is not that words do not matter, but that **non-verbal cues strongly influence how emotional messages are interpreted**.

### 4.3. Non-verbal communication and culture

Furthermore, non-verbal communication is **culturally bound**. Gestures, distance and eye contact vary from one culture to another. For example, the gesture that means *"OK"* in some countries can be offensive in others, and in some cultures children show respect by avoiding eye contact with adults. Consequently, teachers should be aware of these differences, especially in multicultural classrooms, and introduce them as part of **intercultural competence**.

## 5. Extralinguistic strategies: non-verbal reactions to messages in different contexts

Having described verbal and non-verbal communication, we can now look at how teachers use extralinguistic strategies, and how pupils react non-verbally to messages, which is the final part of the title.

### 5.1. Total Physical Response

Firstly, **Total Physical Response**, developed by **Asher (1969)**, is based on the idea that children can show understanding through **physical responses** before they are ready to speak. Pupils follow commands such as *"Stand up"*, *"Touch your nose"* or *"Walk like an elephant"*. In this way, they react non-verbally to verbal messages, which lowers anxiety and allows the teacher to check understanding immediately. Games such as *Simon says* are a classic example.

### 5.2. Gestures, mime, visual support and realia

Secondly, teachers support their verbal messages with **gestures, mime, facial expressions, flashcards, pictures and realia** (real objects). For example, when telling a story, the teacher can mime *"The giant was very angry"* with a frown and big steps, so that pupils understand the meaning without translation. Over time, pupils also learn to use these strategies themselves when they lack words, for instance by pointing or miming to keep communication going, which is part of **strategic competence** (see topic 3).

### 5.3. Reading pupils' non-verbal reactions

Thirdly, communication works in both directions, so teachers must also **read pupils' non-verbal reactions**. Puzzled faces, silence, restlessness or lack of eye contact often reveal that a message has not been understood. Teachers can make this feedback systematic with simple **non-verbal routines**: thumbs up, sideways or down to show understanding; **traffic-light cards** (green: *I understand*, amber: *I'm not sure*, red: *I need help*); or mini-whiteboards to show answers. For example, after explaining a game, the teacher asks for thumbs and quickly sees who needs another demonstration.

### 5.4. Non-verbal reactions in different contexts

Finally, the title mentions **different contexts**, because non-verbal reactions change with the situation. In a **story**, pupils show emotions and act out characters; in a **game**, they react physically to instructions; in a **role-play** in a shop, they practise culturally appropriate gestures, such as smiling, nodding or waiting their turn; and in a **video call** with a partner school, they learn that waving and clear facial expressions are essential when the channel is limited.

## 6. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, not all pupils interpret or use non-verbal communication in the same way. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, teachers should adapt their communication. Pupils with **autism spectrum disorder** may need **explicit teaching** of non-verbal cues, such as what a facial expression means, supported by pictures. Pupils with a **developmental language disorder** benefit from gestures and visuals that reinforce words. Pupils with **hearing loss** need to see the teacher's face clearly and receive written or visual support, and **newly arrived pupils** rely heavily on gestures, routines and a buddy during their first weeks.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**. Through an **observation scale**, teachers can record how pupils respond to instructions, use communication strategies and participate in classroom routines, which is especially useful in the first cycle, when written tests are not appropriate.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the first day of school, when the whole class sat down after hearing a sentence they had never heard before. That moment shows that, in the foreign language classroom, **communication is the result of words, gestures, tone and context working together**. As we have seen, verbal communication provides the input pupils need, while non-verbal communication and extralinguistic strategies make that input understandable and allow pupils to respond before they can speak. Our challenge as teachers is to **use our whole body as a teaching tool** and to read our pupils' reactions carefully, so that every child, from the very first day, feels able to communicate in English.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Classroom as communicative context: input (**Krashen, 1982**), interaction (**Long, 1996**), output (**Swain, 1985**).
> - Teacher talk = main input (**Cameron, 2001**); English as main language; classroom language; affective filter.
> - Non-verbal: kinesics, proxemics (**Hall, 1966**), paralanguage, appearance (**Knapp et al., 2014**); culturally bound.
> - Mehrabian's 7-38-55 % applies only to feelings and attitudes (**Mehrabian, 1971**): not a general rule.
> - Extralinguistic strategies: TPR (**Asher, 1969**), gestures, mime, visuals, realia; reading pupils' reactions; thumbs and traffic lights.
