## 1. Introduction

Marcos, a ten-year-old pupil, used to say that English was "boring" and "useless". He rarely participated and often forgot his homework. One day, the class started a project in which each group interviewed a favourite sports star in English, with one pupil playing the star. Marcos chose a famous English footballer, watched real interviews to prepare, and spent the whole week practising questions such as *"How do you feel before a match?"*. For the first time, he asked to speak in front of the class. Nothing had changed in Marcos's ability; what had changed was that the task was **about him**: his interests, his choices and his goals. This transformation illustrates the subject of this topic.

Indeed, the current curriculum places the pupil at the centre of the learning process. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha promote **competence-based**, **active** and **inclusive** learning, through **learning situations** connected with pupils' interests and lives, and they highlight the key competence **personal, social and learning to learn** (CPSAA), which includes motivation, self-regulation and self-assessment.

To develop this topic, I will first explain the theoretical foundations of student-centred teaching and its main applications. Then I will analyse the types of motivation and attitudes towards English, and how to identify them. Finally, I will propose practical applications to build motivation, and I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. The student-centred teaching and learning process: foundations

### 2.1. Humanism

To begin with, **student-centred** teaching places pupils' needs, interests and abilities at the heart of planning, instead of the textbook or the teacher's explanation. One of its main roots is **humanistic psychology**. **Rogers (1969)**, in *Freedom to Learn*, argued that significant learning occurs when it is **personally relevant** to the learner, when the learner participates responsibly in the process, and when the teacher acts as a **facilitator** in a climate of trust and acceptance. In the English classroom, this means valuing pupils' feelings and creating a safe environment in which mistakes are accepted.

### 2.2. Constructivism

Secondly, **constructivism** explains that learners **actively build** their knowledge. **Piaget** showed that children construct understanding through interaction with their environment, and **Vygotsky (1978)** emphasised the role of social interaction and the **zone of proximal development**, the distance between what a child can do alone and with help. Consequently, teachers should start from pupils' prior knowledge and provide **scaffolding** that is gradually withdrawn.

### 2.3. Self-determination and self-efficacy

Thirdly, motivation research offers a solid foundation. **Ryan and Deci (2000)**, in their **self-determination theory**, showed that people are more intrinsically motivated when three basic psychological needs are satisfied: **autonomy** (feeling that they have choice), **competence** (feeling capable) and **relatedness** (feeling connected to others). In addition, **Bandura (1977)** introduced the concept of **self-efficacy**, the belief in one's ability to succeed in a task, which grows above all through **experiences of success**. For example, a pupil who manages to order food in a role-play feels more confident about speaking English next time.

## 3. Applications of the student-centred approach

Having seen the foundations, let us now consider how they are applied in the classroom.

Firstly, pupils should have opportunities for **choice and autonomy**: choosing the topic of a project, the book they read or the way they present their work. Secondly, teachers should develop **metacognition**, which **Flavell (1979)** defined as knowledge and regulation of one's own thinking. Pupils can learn to plan, monitor and evaluate their learning, for example by setting a personal goal at the beginning of a unit (*"I want to learn to talk about my hobbies"*) and checking it at the end. In addition, pupils can take on **responsibilities** in the classroom, such as being the weather reporter, the helper who hands out materials or the "English monitor" who reminds classmates to use classroom language, which strengthens their sense of autonomy and belonging. Thirdly, **feedback** must help pupils to progress: **Hattie and Timperley (2007)** showed that effective feedback answers three questions: *Where am I going? How am I going? Where to next?*. Finally, **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) offers multiple ways of engaging, accessing information and expressing learning.

However, two clarifications are essential. On the one hand, student-centred teaching does **not** mean adapting teaching to supposed **learning styles**, such as "visual" or "auditory" learners: the review by **Pashler et al. (2008)** found no adequate evidence that matching instruction to learning styles improves learning. On the other hand, student-centred does **not** mean **unguided**: young learners still need clear explanations, models and structured practice from the teacher. The pupil is at the centre, but the teacher remains the guide.

## 4. Motivations and attitudes towards English

Since motivation is at the heart of this approach, the second part of the title asks us to identify pupils' motivations and attitudes towards English.

### 4.1. Types of motivation

Several models describe motivation to learn a language. **Gardner (1985)** distinguished between **integrative motivation**, the wish to connect with speakers of the language and their culture, and **instrumental motivation**, the wish to obtain a practical benefit, such as a good job. Self-determination theory (**Ryan & Deci, 2000**) distinguishes between **intrinsic motivation**, doing something because it is enjoyable or interesting, and **extrinsic motivation**, doing it to obtain a reward or avoid a punishment. In Primary, intrinsic motivation, linked to fun, curiosity and success, is especially powerful.

More recently, **Dörnyei (2009)** proposed the **L2 Motivational Self System**, according to which learners are motivated by their **ideal L2 self**, the image of the English speaker they would like to become; their **ought-to L2 self**, what they think others expect of them; and their **learning experience**, the quality of their classes. For example, a pupil who imagines herself travelling to London and chatting with new friends has a strong ideal L2 self.

### 4.2. Attitudes and anxiety

In addition, pupils' **attitudes** towards English, its speakers and the class itself influence their learning. Negative attitudes may come from previous failure, from beliefs at home (*"English is too difficult"*) or from **anxiety**. **Horwitz, Horwitz and Cope (1986)** described **foreign language classroom anxiety**, a specific anxiety related to speaking in front of others, fear of negative evaluation and test anxiety, which can block participation. For instance, a pupil who knows the answer but stays silent because they are afraid of making a mistake in front of classmates is showing this kind of anxiety. Fortunately, children's attitudes are still flexible in Primary, so positive experiences can change them quickly, and the teacher's own enthusiasm for English is one of the strongest influences.

## 5. Identifying motivations and attitudes

But how can teachers find out what motivates their pupils? Several instruments, adapted to children's age, can help:
- **Questionnaires** with simple statements and smiley faces (*"I like singing in English" 😀 😐 ☹️*).
- **Short interviews** or informal conversations about their interests and their use of English outside school.
- **Systematic observation** of participation, effort and attitudes during different activities.
- **Learner diaries** or exit tickets in which pupils say what they enjoyed and what was difficult.
- **Drawings**, such as *"Me speaking English"*, which reveal younger pupils' feelings.

For example, at the beginning of the year, a fifth-year teacher discovers through a questionnaire that most pupils enjoy video games and football, but feel nervous about speaking in front of the class, which allows her to plan relevant topics and more pair work.

## 6. Practical applications

Finally, the title asks for practical applications. **Dörnyei (2001)** proposed a set of **motivational strategies** organised in four stages: **creating basic motivational conditions**, such as a pleasant and supportive atmosphere; **generating initial motivation**, by making tasks relevant and increasing pupils' expectation of success; **maintaining and protecting motivation**, through stimulating tasks, achievable goals and protecting pupils' self-esteem; and **encouraging positive self-evaluation**, by giving informative feedback and celebrating effort and progress.

In practice, this means:
- Connecting topics with pupils' **interests** and with **real purposes**, such as eTwinning or projects for real audiences.
- Planning tasks with an appropriate level of **challenge** and plenty of **success**.
- Offering **choice** in topics, roles and products.
- Reducing **anxiety** through pair work before whole-class speaking, rehearsal and positive error correction.
- Promoting **self-assessment** and visible progress, such as *can-do* checklists or portfolios.

**Example (5th year): *"My English passport"***. At the start of the year, each pupil completes a questionnaire about their interests and sets three personal goals. During each term, they choose a mini-project related to their interests, such as a recipe, a song or a video about a hobby. They record their progress in a passport with *can-do* stamps, and at the end of the year they present their favourite project to their families.

## 7. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, motivation is especially important for pupils who find English difficult. Following **Decree 85/2018**, teachers should ensure that pupils with **learning difficulties**, **dyslexia** or **ADHD** experience frequent success through adapted tasks, while pupils with **high ability** need challenge to stay motivated. Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment must be **continuous and formative**, and **self-assessment** and **co-assessment** help pupils to take responsibility for their own learning.

## 8. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to Marcos and his football interview. As we have seen, student-centred teaching is based on solid **foundations**, from humanism and constructivism to **self-determination theory** and **self-efficacy**, and it is applied through **choice, metacognition, feedback and inclusion**, without falling into myths such as learning styles. Identifying pupils' **motivations and attitudes** allows us to design tasks that satisfy their needs for **autonomy, competence and relatedness**. Our challenge as teachers is to place every pupil at the centre, so that, like Marcos, they discover that English is not "useless", but a key to their own interests and dreams.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Foundations: humanism (**Rogers, 1969**); constructivism (Piaget; **Vygotsky, 1978**); autonomy, competence, relatedness (**Ryan & Deci, 2000**); self-efficacy (**Bandura, 1977**).
> - Applications: choice, metacognition (**Flavell, 1979**), feedback (**Hattie & Timperley, 2007**), UDL (**CAST, 2018**); not learning styles (**Pashler et al., 2008**); not unguided.
> - Motivation: integrative vs instrumental (**Gardner, 1985**); intrinsic vs extrinsic; ideal and ought-to L2 self (**Dörnyei, 2009**); language anxiety (**Horwitz et al., 1986**).
> - Instruments: smiley questionnaires, interviews, observation, diaries, drawings.
> - Motivational strategies in four stages (**Dörnyei, 2001**).
