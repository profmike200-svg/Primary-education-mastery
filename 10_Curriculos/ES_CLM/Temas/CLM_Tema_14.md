## 1. Introduction

Imagine two fourth-year classrooms working on the same unit about animals. In the first one, pupils copy a list of words and a grammar rule into their notebooks. In the second, they are planning a visit to the zoo: they ask each other *"Which animals do you want to see?"*, vote, and design a map in English. Both teachers are "teaching English", but **only one of them is teaching pupils to communicate**. The difference lies in the methodology, the subject of this topic.

In fact, methodology is not a neutral choice. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha define the foreign language area through **communicative specific competences**, developed through **learning situations** that are meaningful for pupils. Consequently, the methods and techniques we choose must serve this goal.

With this in mind, I will first clarify the concepts of approach, method and technique and briefly recall the notion of communicative competence. Then I will describe the main methods and techniques focused on communication, and after that I will set out the specific methodological basis for teaching English in Primary. Finally, I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Approach, method and technique

To begin with, it is essential to use these terms precisely. **Anthony (1963)** distinguished three levels: the **approach**, a set of beliefs about the nature of language and learning; the **method**, an overall plan for presenting language based on that approach; and the **technique**, a specific activity carried out in the classroom. For example, the communicative approach (approach) can be put into practice through task-based learning (method), which in turn uses an information-gap activity (technique). Later, **Richards and Rodgers (2014)** refined this model into **approach, design and procedure**, adding elements such as the roles of teachers, learners and materials.

Today, however, many authors question the search for a single perfect method. **Kumaravadivelu (2006)** describes a **postmethod condition**, in which teachers do not follow a fixed recipe but apply **general principles** to their own context. This is sometimes called **principled eclecticism**: choosing techniques from different sources, but always for a reason.

## 3. Communicative competence

But what exactly are these methods trying to achieve? Their goal is **communicative competence**, which is analysed in depth in topic 3. In short, **Hymes (1972)** defined it as knowing not only grammar, but also what to say, to whom, when and how, and **Canale and Swain (1980)** divided it into grammatical, sociolinguistic and strategic competence. Today, the **Council of Europe (2020)** describes communication through **reception, production, interaction and mediation**, the model behind the six specific competences of our curriculum. For example, a pupil who explains a game's rules to a newly arrived classmate is using mediation, a competence that a list of words alone could never develop.

## 4. Methods and techniques focused on communicative competence

Having defined the goal, let us now look at the methods and techniques that pursue it.

### 4.1. Communicative Language Teaching

**Communicative Language Teaching (CLT)** is the general framework of all current methods. Its principles are clear: **meaning comes first**, activities have a **real purpose**, there is an **information gap** that makes communication necessary, and **fluency** is valued alongside accuracy. For example, in a *spot the difference* activity, each pupil has a different picture and they must talk to find the differences. Besides **information gaps**, teachers can create **opinion gaps**, in which pupils share preferences (*Which pet is the best? Why?*), and **reasoning gaps**, in which they deduce new information, such as working out a timetable from several clues. All of them share the same idea: pupils speak because they need to, not only because the teacher asks them to.

### 4.2. Task-based learning

Within CLT, **task-based learning** places a meaningful task at the centre of the lesson. **Willis (1996)** proposed a framework with three phases: a **pre-task**, in which the teacher introduces the topic and useful language; a **task cycle**, in which pupils do the task, plan how to report it and present it; and a **language focus**, in which they analyse and practise specific forms. For instance, pupils plan the zoo visit from the introduction and, afterwards, the teacher highlights *want to* + verb.

### 4.3. Focus on form within communication

Nevertheless, communication alone is not enough. The meta-analysis by **Norris and Ortega (2000)** showed that **explicit instruction**, which draws learners' attention to form, is more effective than purely implicit exposure. Therefore, the best results come from **focus on form within meaningful tasks**: short, clear explanations or games about a structure, followed by its immediate use in communication.

### 4.4. Content and Language Integrated Learning

In addition, **CLIL** teaches subjects through English. **Coyle, Hood and Marsh (2010)** describe it through the **4Cs**: **content, communication, cognition and culture**. In Castilla-La Mancha, it is the basis of bilingual programmes; for example, pupils learn the water cycle in Science while using *First…, then…, finally…* in English. The review by **Pérez-Cañado (2012)** found generally positive effects of CLIL on language learning, although it also warned that more rigorous studies are needed, especially regarding content learning. Therefore, CLIL teachers must make sure that pupils understand the content, using visuals, hands-on experiments and language support.

### 4.5. Techniques especially suited to children

Furthermore, some techniques are particularly effective with young learners:
- **Total Physical Response**, proposed by **Asher (1969)**, in which pupils respond physically to commands (*Jump! Touch your head!*).
- **Storytelling**, which provides rich, repeated and emotional input.
- **Songs, rhymes and chants**, which develop rhythm, pronunciation and memory.
- **Games**, which offer a real reason to use language and plenty of repetition.
- **Drama and role-play**, which simulate real situations, such as a shop or a restaurant.
- **Cooperative learning**: according to **Johnson and Johnson (2009)**, groups are truly cooperative only when there is **positive interdependence** and **individual accountability**, for example by giving each pupil a role.
- **Project-based learning**, which ends in a real product, such as a class magazine.

### 4.6. Digital tools

Finally, **digital tools and artificial intelligence** can provide authentic input, practice and feedback, but only when their use is **purposeful and moderate**. For instance, pupils can record a short podcast about their school for a partner class.

## 5. Specific methodological basis for English language teaching in Primary

Methods and techniques, however, need a solid basis. This brings us to the second part of the title: the specific principles that should guide English teaching in Primary.

### 5.1. Curricular principles

On the one hand, the curriculum establishes several principles: **competence-based learning** through **learning situations**; **Universal Design for Learning**; the **integration of skills**; the **priority of oral language** in the first cycle; and **assessment as part of learning**, as regulated by **Order 185/2022**. In particular, a **learning situation** is a set of activities linked to a meaningful context and leading to a final product, which connects school learning with real life. For instance, preparing a recipe book for a school fair is a learning situation, whereas a list of unrelated exercises about food is not. Learning situations naturally integrate skills, encourage cooperation and allow pupils to apply what they know in new contexts.

### 5.2. Principles from research with young learners

On the other hand, research with children, summarised by **Cameron (2001)** and **Pinter (2017)**, offers seven principles:
1. **Meaning and activity**: children learn by doing, supported by context, routines and visuals.
2. **Rich input**: plenty of comprehensible English before demanding output, with English as the main classroom language.
3. **Scaffolding**: as **Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976)** explained, adults support learners in what they cannot yet do alone and **gradually withdraw** that support, which connects with Vygotsky's **zone of proximal development**. Speaking frames are a good example.
4. **Recycling and spaced review**: **Dunlosky et al. (2013)** found that **spaced practice** and **retrieval practice** are among the most effective learning techniques, so language must reappear in later units.
5. **Explicit focus on form** inside communication (**Norris & Ortega, 2000**).
6. **Positive affective climate**: low anxiety, frequent success and motivation.
7. **Age-appropriate tasks**: concrete, playful and short in the first cycle, more abstract and autonomous in the third.

**Example (6th year): *"Our Town Guide for Visitors"*.** Pupils read real tourist leaflets (**input**), decide in groups which places to include (**interaction** and **cooperative learning**), study how to give directions (**focus on form**), write and record their guide (**production** and **digital tools**) and explain it to a partner school (**mediation**). In a single learning situation, CLT, task-based learning and the seven principles work together.

## 6. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, all these methods must reach every pupil. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, teachers can use flexible grouping and adapt techniques: pupils with **ADHD** benefit from movement and short tasks; pupils with **dyslexia** from oral priority; **newly arrived pupils** from TPR and visuals; and pupils with **high ability** from open tasks with extra challenge. Likewise, assessment must be **continuous and formative**, using observation scales, rubrics and ***can-do* self-assessment** linked to the tasks. For example, in the town guide project, a rubric shared with pupils from the beginning can assess content, language, cooperation and presentation, while a short self-assessment (*I can give directions to the station*) helps pupils to become aware of their progress. In this way, assessment becomes part of the methodology rather than a separate event at the end of the unit.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the two classrooms working on animals. The difference between them is not the topic, but the methodology. As we have seen, **there is no magic method**; instead, research and the curriculum offer a set of **principles**: rich input, real communication, explicit attention to form, scaffolding, recycling and a safe classroom. Choosing the **right technique for each moment and each child** is the teacher's professional skill, and it is what turns a list of animals into a real conversation about a trip to the zoo.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Approach → method → technique (**Anthony, 1963**); approach, design, procedure (**Richards & Rodgers, 2014**); postmethod (**Kumaravadivelu, 2006**).
> - Goal: communicative competence (**Hymes, 1972**; **Canale & Swain, 1980**; **Council of Europe, 2020**).
> - CLT; TBL: pre-task, task cycle, language focus (**Willis, 1996**); focus on form (**Norris & Ortega, 2000**); CLIL 4Cs (**Coyle et al., 2010**).
> - Child-friendly techniques: TPR (**Asher, 1969**), stories, songs, games, drama, cooperative learning (**Johnson & Johnson, 2009**), projects.
> - Seven principles (**Cameron, 2001**; **Pinter, 2017**): meaning, input, scaffolding (**Wood et al., 1976**), spaced recycling (**Dunlosky et al., 2013**), focus on form, climate, age.
