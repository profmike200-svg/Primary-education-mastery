## 1. Introduction

For weeks, a shy first-year pupil has hardly said a word in English. One morning, the teacher reads Bill Martin Jr. and Eric Carle's *Brown Bear, Brown Bear, What Do You See?* for the third time. When she turns the page and asks *"Red Bird, Red Bird, what do you see?"*, the shy pupil answers loudly, together with the rest of the class: *"I see a yellow duck looking at me!"*. The repetition, the rhythm and the pictures have done what no exercise had achieved. This is the potential of children's literature, the subject of this topic.

Indeed, the current curriculum recognises this potential. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha include the comprehension of **simple literary texts**, such as stories, rhymes and songs, among the basic knowledge of the foreign language area, and they promote **reading for pleasure** and the development of **reading habits** throughout Primary. Literature thus contributes to the specific competences of comprehension, production and interculturality.

To develop this topic, I will first define children's literature and its benefits. Then I will explain how to use it to develop oral comprehension, to promote reading habits and to appreciate the poetic function of language, following the three parts of the title. Finally, I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Children's literature in English

### 2.1. Concept

To begin with, **children's literature** can be defined as the texts written for, or adopted by, children, including nursery rhymes, folk tales, picture books, poems, plays, comics and novels. Its periods and authors are described in topic 15; here the focus is on its **didactic use**.

### 2.2. Benefits

Children's literature offers many benefits for language learning. Firstly, it provides **rich, contextualised input**, with repetition and predictable patterns that make language memorable. Secondly, it increases **motivation**, because children love stories and want to know what happens next. Thirdly, it connects with **emotions and values**, such as friendship or courage. Fourthly, it offers a window onto **other cultures**. Finally, it develops **imagination and creativity**, which pupils can later use in their own productions. For example, after reading a story about a monster who is afraid of the dark, pupils not only learn vocabulary about the house and feelings, but also talk about their own fears and invent ways of helping the monster.

### 2.3. Selection criteria

However, not every book is suitable. **Ellis and Brewster (2014)** suggest considering the **language level**, the presence of **repetition** and **visual support**, the **interest and age** of the pupils, the **cultural content**, and the **potential for activities** across the curriculum. For example, *The Very Hungry Caterpillar* is ideal for the first cycle, because it combines days, numbers and food with very clear illustrations.

## 3. Didactic applications to develop oral comprehension

Having established why and how to choose books, let us now look at the first didactic application in the title: **oral comprehension**.

### 3.1. Storytelling techniques

In Primary, stories are first **listened to** rather than read. **Wright (2009)** shows that good storytelling relies on the **voice** (changing tone for each character), **gestures and mime**, **pictures and props**, **eye contact** and **pupil participation**, for instance by inviting them to repeat a refrain or make sound effects. Telling a story several times, with growing participation, turns comprehension into production. Preparation is also essential: the teacher should rehearse the story, simplify difficult language without losing its rhythm, and decide in advance where to pause, which questions to ask and which words pupils will repeat. For instance, in *Goldilocks and the Three Bears*, pupils can use a deep, a normal and a tiny voice for each bear, which makes the story memorable and practises intonation at the same time.

### 3.2. Before, during and after the story

Furthermore, storytelling becomes more effective when it is organised in three stages, as **Ellis and Brewster (2014)** propose. **Before** the story, the teacher activates prior knowledge, introduces key words with flashcards and asks pupils to predict from the cover. **During** the story, pupils point, hold up cards, join in with the refrain or answer simple questions. **After** the story, they sequence pictures, act out scenes, retell the story or create a new ending.

### 3.3. Dialogic reading

In addition, shared reading can become a conversation. In **dialogic reading**, developed by **Whitehurst et al. (1988)**, the adult does not just read, but encourages the child to talk about the book through the **PEER** sequence: **P**rompt the child to say something, **E**valuate the answer, **E**xpand it and **R**epeat the prompt. The prompts follow the acronym **CROWD**: **C**ompletion (*"The caterpillar was still…"*), **R**ecall, **O**pen-ended, **W**h- questions and **D**istancing, which links the story to the child's life (*"What do you eat on Saturdays?"*). The meta-analysis by **Mol et al. (2008)** found that dialogic reading has positive effects on young children's oral language, especially vocabulary.

**Example (2nd year).** The teacher tells *The Very Hungry Caterpillar* with a puppet and real fruit. During the second reading, pupils hold up the fruit card when they hear it and join in with *"but he was still hungry!"*. Finally, they create a class book about *The Very Hungry Class*, with each pupil drawing and saying what they eat.

## 4. Promoting reading habits

However, our goal is not only that pupils understand stories in class, but that they become **readers**, which is the second part of the title.

### 4.1. What research says

Research strongly supports reading for pleasure. In a classic study in Fiji, **Elley and Mangubhai (1983)** found that pupils who received a **"book flood"** of attractive English storybooks improved their English reading and listening more than pupils following a traditional programme. More generally, the meta-analysis by **Mol and Bus (2011)** showed that frequent reading is associated with better comprehension, vocabulary and spelling, in a **reciprocal** relationship: good readers read more, and reading more makes them better readers.

### 4.2. Extensive reading in Primary

Consequently, teachers should promote **extensive reading**. **Day and Bamford (1998)** describe its key principles: easy material, wide variety, free choice, a lot of reading, and reading as its own reward. The key condition is **ease**: according to **Nation (2013)**, pleasure reading requires knowing about **98 % of the words**. Practical resources include:
- A **class library** with picture books, graded readers, comics and non-fiction.
- A regular **reading time** in which everyone, including the teacher, reads.
- A **reading passport** or reading tree where pupils record the books they have read.
- **Book recommendations** by pupils, such as short oral reviews or posters.
- **Family involvement**: lending bags with a book and an audio recording, so that families can share the story at home even if they do not speak English.
- **Digital libraries** and audiobooks, which offer a correct pronunciation model and allow pupils to read along at their own pace.

In addition, graded readers are organised by **levels**, so pupils can progress gradually and experience success. The teacher's role is to help each pupil find a book that is easy and interesting enough, and then to protect reading time from constant tasks and questions.

## 5. Appreciating the poetic function of language

Finally, the title invites us to go beyond comprehension and habits, and to help pupils **appreciate the beauty of language**.

According to **Jakobson (1960)**, the **poetic function** appears when the message focuses on **its own form**: the sound, rhythm and arrangement of words become important in themselves. Children are naturally sensitive to this function, as their love of nursery rhymes and tongue twisters shows. In the English classroom, teachers can help pupils to notice:
- **Rhyme**: *cat, hat, mat*, as in Dr. Seuss's books.
- **Rhythm**: the regular beat of rhymes and chants.
- **Repetition**: refrains such as *"Run, run, as fast as you can!"*.
- **Alliteration**: *Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers*.
- **Onomatopoeia and wordplay**: *swishy swashy*, *splash splosh*.

Moreover, pupils can **create** their own poetic texts: acrostic poems with their names, new verses for a rhyme, shape poems or simple haiku. For example, after reading a poem about colours, third-year pupils can write *"Red is a strawberry, red is my nose in winter"*, playing with images and sound. Finally, the class can **perform** their poems in a small poetry recital for families, using rhythm, gestures and intonation, which connects the poetic function with pronunciation and with pupils' self-confidence.

## 6. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, literature must reach every child. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, teachers can offer **different formats** (picture books, audiobooks, digital stories, wordless books), allow **choice**, and select books in which all pupils can see themselves reflected, as well as books that open windows onto other lives. Pupils with **dyslexia** benefit from listening while following the text, and **newly arrived pupils** from wordless picture books and bilingual editions.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**. Teachers can assess comprehension through **retelling** with pictures, **observation** during storytelling, **reading records** and short creative productions, always protecting the pleasure of reading by avoiding constant testing.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the shy pupil who finally said *"I see a yellow duck looking at me!"*. That moment shows that children's literature can unlock comprehension, confidence and joy. As we have seen, stories develop **oral comprehension** through storytelling and dialogic reading, **reading habits** through easy, varied and freely chosen books, and an appreciation of the **poetic function** through rhyme, rhythm and play. Our challenge as teachers is to fill our classrooms with stories, so that every child discovers that reading in English is not a task, but a pleasure that can last a lifetime.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Benefits: input, motivation, emotions, culture, creativity; selection criteria (**Ellis & Brewster, 2014**).
> - Oral comprehension: storytelling techniques (**Wright, 2009**); before/during/after; dialogic reading, PEER + CROWD (**Whitehurst et al., 1988**; **Mol et al., 2008**).
> - Reading habits: book flood (**Elley & Mangubhai, 1983**); print exposure (**Mol & Bus, 2011**); extensive reading (**Day & Bamford, 1998**); 98 % coverage (**Nation, 2013**).
> - Poetic function: focus on the message itself (**Jakobson, 1960**): rhyme, rhythm, repetition, alliteration, wordplay; pupils create poems.
