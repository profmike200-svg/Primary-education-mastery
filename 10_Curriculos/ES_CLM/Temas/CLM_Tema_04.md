## 1. Introduction

On a Friday morning, a fourth-year class in Toledo connects by video call with two partner schools, one in Kenya and one in Poland. None of the children speak each other's mother tongue, yet for twenty minutes they introduce themselves, show their favourite toys and sing a song together **in English**. At the end, the Polish children teach everyone to say *"cześć"* and the Kenyan children say *"jambo"*. In that small call, English has worked as a **bridge between peoples**, and at the same time it has awakened **curiosity about other languages**. These two ideas are the heart of this topic.

Indeed, the current curriculum gives great importance to both aspects. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)** and **Royal Decree 157/2022** include **plurilingual competence** among the key competences, and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha includes **plurilingualism** and **interculturality** among the six specific competences of the foreign language area. In other words, the aim is not only to learn English, but also to value **linguistic and cultural diversity** and to use languages to understand others.

To develop this topic, I will first analyse the foreign language as a means of communication among people and nations, with special attention to English as a global language. Then I will explore linguistic diversity and the concepts of plurilingual and intercultural competence. After that, I will propose classroom applications and refer to diversity and assessment, before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. The foreign language as a means of communication among people and nations

### 2.1. Overcoming the language barrier

To begin with, it is worth remembering that the world is linguistically divided: when two people do not share a language, communication becomes difficult. Throughout history, humanity has found three main ways of overcoming this **language barrier**. Firstly, the use of a **lingua franca**, a common language used by speakers of different languages, such as Latin in medieval Europe or English today. There have also been attempts to create artificial languages, such as **Esperanto**, but none has achieved wide use. Secondly, **translation and interpretation**, which today include automatic translation and artificial intelligence. Although these tools are increasingly powerful, they cannot replace the personal relationship, the cultural understanding and the cognitive benefits of learning a language. Thirdly, **plurilingualism**, the ability of individuals to communicate in several languages, which is the option promoted by the European Union and by our curriculum.

### 2.2. Why English? English as a global language

But why has English become the main lingua franca of our time? According to **Crystal (2003)**, a language becomes global not because of its intrinsic qualities, but because of the **power of its speakers**: first the political and military power of the British Empire, and later the economic, scientific and technological influence of the United States. Today, English is the dominant language of international communication, science, business, aviation, the internet and popular culture, and **non-native speakers clearly outnumber native speakers**.

To describe this situation, **Kachru (1985)** proposed a model of **three concentric circles**:
- The **inner circle**, where English is the first language of most people, such as the United Kingdom, the United States, Ireland, Canada, Australia and New Zealand.
- The **outer circle**, where English is a second or official language with an important institutional role, as in India, Nigeria, Singapore or the Philippines.
- The **expanding circle**, where English is learned as a foreign language, as in Spain, China, Brazil or Poland.

This model has important consequences for teaching. Since most interactions in English now take place between non-native speakers, the goal is not to imitate a native speaker perfectly, but to communicate **intelligibly** with people from all over the world. For example, our pupils in Toledo are more likely to use English with a Polish or Kenyan friend than with a British one.

## 3. Generating an interest in linguistic diversity

However, the importance of English must not make us forget the second part of the title: generating an interest in **linguistic diversity**.

### 3.1. Linguistic diversity in the world, in Europe and in Spain

There are around **7,000 languages** in the world, many of them in danger of disappearing. In Europe, the European Union has 24 official languages and has set the goal that every citizen should learn **two languages in addition to their mother tongue**. Spain itself is a plurilingual country: alongside Spanish, several languages are **co-official** in their autonomous communities, such as Catalan, Galician, Basque and Valencian. In fact, the **Spanish Constitution (1978)**, in its article 3, states that the richness of Spain's different linguistic modalities is a **cultural heritage** that deserves special respect and protection.

### 3.2. The languages of our pupils

Moreover, linguistic diversity is not something distant: it is present in our classrooms. Many pupils speak other languages at home, such as Arabic, Romanian, Chinese or Ukrainian. These languages should be seen as a **resource**, not as a problem. Research has shown that growing up with several languages **does not confuse children**; on the contrary, bilingual children develop each language normally when they receive enough input. Therefore, when a newly arrived pupil teaches the class how to count in Arabic, the whole group learns that languages are valuable, and the pupil feels recognised.

## 4. Plurilingual and intercultural competence

Building on this diversity, current European frameworks propose two key competences, which give a theoretical basis to everything we have discussed.

### 4.1. Plurilingual and pluricultural competence

The **Council of Europe (2020)**, in the *Companion Volume* of the Common European Framework of Reference, describes **plurilingual and pluricultural competence** as the ability to use a repertoire of languages and cultural experiences, in which all languages are connected and interact, even when the speaker's knowledge of some of them is partial. For example, a pupil who recognises that *hospital*, *hôpital* and *Krankenhaus* refer to the same place, or who explains in Spanish what an English sign says, is using this competence. This idea also connects with **mediation**, one of the communicative activities of the CEFR. For instance, during a school trip, a pupil who helps a tourist by translating a simple sign or a short explanation is putting all their languages at the service of communication between people.

Similarly, **García and Li Wei (2014)** use the term **translanguaging** to describe how plurilingual speakers use all their languages flexibly to make meaning. In the classroom, this does not mean abandoning English as the main language, but accepting that pupils' other languages can support their learning.

### 4.2. Intercultural communicative competence

At the same time, learning a language means entering another culture. **Byram (1997)** defined **intercultural communicative competence** as the ability to communicate with people from other cultures, and described it through several components: **attitudes** of curiosity and openness; **knowledge** of one's own and other cultures; **skills of interpreting and relating** cultural products; **skills of discovery and interaction**; and **critical cultural awareness**. For instance, when pupils compare how birthdays are celebrated in Spain, the United Kingdom and India, they are not only learning vocabulary, but also discovering that there are different, equally valid ways of doing things.

### 4.3. Teaching language and culture without stereotypes

Consequently, culture should be taught carefully. Teachers should **avoid stereotypes**, such as presenting all British people as tea drinkers, and should show the **diversity** of the English-speaking world, including countries of the outer circle. In addition, comparisons should start from the pupils' own culture and should promote **respect and curiosity** rather than judgement.

## 5. Classroom applications

Moving from theory to practice, there are many ways of developing these competences in Primary:
- A **language wall** where pupils display words in all the languages spoken by the class and their families.
- Greeting routines in different languages, such as *"Hello"* in the language of the day.
- Discovering **cognates** and **false friends**, for example *actual* and *actually*, which develops language awareness.
- **eTwinning** projects and video calls with schools in other countries, where English is the shared language.
- Stories, songs and games from different cultures and from different English-speaking countries.
- Intercultural projects, such as a class book about festivals around the world.

**Example (4th year): *"Hello from around the world"*.** Pupils investigate how children greet each other and what they eat for breakfast in four countries, one of them from the outer circle, such as India. In groups, they prepare a poster in English and add a greeting in each country's language. Finally, they share their posters with a partner school through eTwinning and compare the results with their own customs. This learning situation develops communication, mediation, plurilingualism and interculturality at the same time.

## 6. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, these activities must include every pupil. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, the languages and cultures of **newly arrived pupils** should be valued as resources, and pupils with special educational needs can participate through visual materials, pictograms and roles adapted to their strengths. For example, a pupil with limited Spanish can be the "expert" who teaches the class greetings in their language.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**. Teachers can use an **observation scale** to record attitudes of respect and curiosity, and pupils can keep a simple **language portfolio**, inspired by the European Language Portfolio, in which they record the languages they know and their intercultural experiences.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the video call between Toledo, Kenya and Poland. In those twenty minutes, English worked as a **bridge between peoples**, and the children also discovered *"cześć"* and *"jambo"*. As we have seen, English is today the main **lingua franca** of the world, but learning it should open pupils' minds to **all languages and cultures**, starting with those present in their own classroom. Our challenge as teachers is to help our pupils become **plurilingual and intercultural citizens**, able to communicate with anyone and curious about everyone.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Overcoming the language barrier: lingua franca, translation, plurilingualism.
> - English is global because of its speakers' power (**Crystal, 2003**); inner, outer and expanding circles (**Kachru, 1985**); intelligibility as the goal.
> - ~7,000 languages; EU "mother tongue + 2"; Spain's co-official languages (**Spanish Constitution, 1978**, art. 3); home languages as resources.
> - Plurilingual and pluricultural competence (**Council of Europe, 2020**); translanguaging (**García & Li Wei, 2014**).
> - Intercultural communicative competence: attitudes, knowledge, skills, critical awareness (**Byram, 1997**).
