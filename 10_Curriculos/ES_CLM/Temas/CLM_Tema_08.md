## 1. Introduction

In a second-year class, Pablo slowly reads aloud a sentence from a picture book: *"The cat is on the mat"*. He sounds out *c-a-t*, looks at the picture and smiles because he has understood. A few weeks later, he takes his pencil and writes his own sentence under a drawing: *"The dog is on the bed"*. In a short time, Pablo has gone from **decoding** someone else's words to **producing** his own text. This journey, from the first contact with print to the creation of texts, is the subject of this topic.

Indeed, written language plays an increasingly important role throughout Primary. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha include **written comprehension and production** within the specific competences of the foreign language area, while recognising that, especially in the first cycle, written language must be built on **oral foundations**. Moreover, reading and writing in English contribute to the key competence in **linguistic communication** and to reading habits across the curriculum.

To develop this topic, I will first describe the features of written language and its place in Primary. Then I will analyse the reading-writing process in three phases: approximation, maturation and improvement. After that, I will distinguish between intensive and extensive reading and explain how writing develops from interpretation to production. Finally, I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Written language and its place in Primary

### 2.1. Features of written language

To begin with, written language differs from spoken language in several ways. It is **permanent**, so readers can go back and re-read; it is usually **planned** and more **explicit**, because the reader is absent and cannot ask questions; and it relies on **spelling, punctuation and layout** instead of intonation and gestures. For example, a pupil writing a postcard must include all the information, because the receiver cannot ask *"Where are you?"*.

### 2.2. Oral before written

For young learners, written language should come **after** oral familiarity with words. As **Cameron (2001)** explains, pupils should read words they already know orally, so that reading becomes a matter of linking a known sound and meaning to a written form. For instance, pupils sing a song about colours for several lessons before matching the written words to coloured cards.

### 2.3. An opaque spelling system

However, reading in English presents a particular challenge for Spanish pupils. Spanish has a **transparent orthography**, in which each letter usually represents the same sound, whereas English is **opaque**: the letters *ough* sound different in *though*, *through* and *tough*. A comparative study by **Seymour, Aro and Erskine (2003)** found that children learning to read in English take considerably longer to master basic decoding than children learning in more transparent languages. Consequently, English reading requires **explicit teaching** of sound-spelling relationships (see topic 10).

## 3. Approximation, maturation and improvement of the reading-writing process

Having described the features of written language, let us now follow the three phases mentioned in the title.

### 3.1. Approximation

The first phase is the **approximation** to written language, in which pupils discover that print carries meaning. Since Spanish pupils already know how to read in their mother tongue, they bring valuable skills, such as knowing that we read from left to right. In English, this phase includes **print awareness** with labels, posters and picture books, and the first **sound-letter links** through **phonics**, for example discovering that *s* says /s/ in *snake*, *sun* and *six*. Shared reading of **big books**, in which the teacher points at the words while reading aloud, is a powerful technique at this stage.

### 3.2. Maturation

The second phase is **maturation**, in which reading becomes more automatic. **Ehri (2005)** describes how children move through **phases of word reading**: from a **pre-alphabetic** phase, in which they recognise words by visual cues, such as the shape of a logo; to a **partial alphabetic** phase, using some letters; then a **full alphabetic** phase, decoding all the letters; and finally a **consolidated alphabetic** phase, recognising chunks such as *-ight* or *-ing* automatically. As decoding becomes fluent, pupils can devote their attention to meaning.

This idea is captured by the **Simple View of Reading**, proposed by **Gough and Tunmer (1986)**: **reading comprehension is the product of decoding and language comprehension**. In other words, a pupil who can decode *"The elephant is enormous"* but does not know what *enormous* means will not understand the sentence, and vice versa. Therefore, in a foreign language, teaching decoding and building oral vocabulary must go hand in hand.

### 3.3. Improvement

The third phase is **improvement**, in which pupils become autonomous, strategic readers and writers. **Castles, Rastle and Nation (2018)** explain that, once decoding is secure, progress depends on reading a lot and building knowledge and vocabulary. In the third cycle, pupils can learn **reading strategies**, such as predicting from the title, guessing words from context, or skimming and scanning, and they begin to read for different purposes, from following instructions to enjoying a story. For example, before reading a leaflet about a zoo, pupils can **skim** it to say what kind of text it is, and then **scan** it to find the opening time.

## 4. Extensive and intensive reading

Once pupils can read, the question is how to organise reading in the classroom, and the title distinguishes two complementary types.

### 4.1. Intensive reading

**Intensive reading** means working **in detail** with a short text, usually with specific tasks. For example, pupils read a short email from a partner school, answer true/false questions, underline the verbs in the past and identify how the email begins and ends. Intensive reading develops comprehension strategies and attention to language, and it normally follows three stages: **pre-reading**, to activate knowledge and set a purpose; **while-reading**, with a clear task; and **post-reading**, to use the information in a new way.

### 4.2. Extensive reading

**Extensive reading**, by contrast, means reading **large amounts of easy texts for pleasure**. **Day and Bamford (1998)** describe its principles: the material is easy, there is a wide variety, learners choose what they read, they read as much as possible, and reading is its own reward. The key condition is **ease**: **Nation (2013)** points out that pleasure reading requires knowing around **98 % of the words** in a text, that is, only one unknown word in fifty. That is why **graded readers**, picture books and a well-stocked **class library** are essential. For example, every Friday, pupils can choose a book from the class library and record it in a simple reading passport.

## 5. Writing: from interpretation to production of texts

Just as reading progresses through phases, writing develops from interpreting written models to producing original texts, which is the final part of the title.

### 5.1. From copying to free writing

In Primary, writing develops through three stages. First, **copying** words and short sentences, always meaningful ones, such as labelling a drawing. Second, **guided writing**, in which pupils complete sentences, follow a model or use a **writing frame** (*My name is… I live in… I like…*). Third, **free writing**, in which pupils produce their own texts, such as a short story or a description of their favourite animal. In this way, pupils first interpret a text and then use it as a springboard to create their own.

### 5.2. Writing as a process

Moreover, writing is not just a product, but a **process**. **Flower and Hayes (1981)** described writing as a set of cognitive processes: **planning**, **translating ideas into text** and **reviewing**. In the classroom, this means teaching pupils to plan with a mind map, write a first draft, revise it with a simple checklist and publish a final version. The meta-analysis by **Graham et al. (2012)** shows that **explicit teaching of writing strategies**, using models and gradually transferring responsibility to pupils, clearly improves the quality of children's writing. Feedback is also part of the process: with a simple routine such as **"two stars and a wish"**, a partner points out two things they liked and one thing to improve, which makes revision concrete and positive.

**Example (5th year).** Pupils read a model postcard (**interpretation**), identify its parts and useful expressions, plan their own postcard with a frame, write a draft, check it with a partner using a checklist, and send the final version to a partner school through **eTwinning** (**production**). Having a real reader gives them a real reason to write carefully.

## 6. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, reading and writing in English can be particularly challenging for some pupils. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, pupils with **dyslexia** benefit from oral priority, systematic phonics, audio versions of texts, dyslexia-friendly fonts and no penalty for spelling errors when the focus is content. Pupils with a **developmental language disorder** need strong oral vocabulary work before reading, and **newly arrived pupils** may need support with the Latin alphabet. Pupils with high ability can read longer graded readers or write extended texts.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**. **Rubrics** shared in advance, **reading records**, checklists for self-assessment and a **writing portfolio** that collects drafts and final versions allow pupils to see their progress throughout the year.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to Pablo, who moved from sounding out *"The cat is on the mat"* to writing *"The dog is on the bed"*. As we have seen, reading and writing in English develop through **approximation**, **maturation** and **improvement**, built on oral foundations and on explicit teaching of the code. **Intensive reading** develops strategies, **extensive reading** creates readers, and writing grows **from interpretation to production**. Our challenge as teachers is to guide every pupil along this path, so that, like Pablo, they discover the pleasure of reading and the power of writing their own words in English.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Oral before written (**Cameron, 2001**); English is opaque (**Seymour et al., 2003**) → explicit phonics.
> - Phases: pre-alphabetic → partial → full → consolidated alphabetic (**Ehri, 2005**); comprehension = decoding × language comprehension (**Gough & Tunmer, 1986**); improvement through reading and knowledge (**Castles et al., 2018**).
> - Intensive (detailed tasks, pre/while/post) vs extensive reading (easy, chosen, for pleasure: **Day & Bamford, 1998**); 98 % coverage (**Nation, 2013**).
> - Writing: copying → guided → free; planning, translating, reviewing (**Flower & Hayes, 1981**); strategy instruction (**Graham et al., 2012**).
