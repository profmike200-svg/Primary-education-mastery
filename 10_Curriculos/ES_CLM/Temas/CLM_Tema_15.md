## 1. Introduction

The teacher opens a picture book and starts chanting: *"We're going on a bear hunt. We're going to catch a big one…"*. Within minutes, the whole second-year class is on its feet, wading through the grass (*swishy swashy!*), splashing through the river and tiptoeing into the cave. When the bear finally appears, everyone runs back to their chairs screaming and laughing. Michael Rosen's book, first published in 1989, has turned a group of seven-year-olds into enthusiastic users of English. This is the power of literature, and choosing the right texts is the subject of this topic.

Indeed, literature has a clear place in the current curriculum. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha include among the basic knowledge of the foreign language area the comprehension of **simple literary texts** and different **text types**, and they encourage reading for pleasure and the appreciation of other cultures. Therefore, teachers need to know which periods, authors and genres are suitable for Primary and how texts can be classified.

To develop this topic, I will first explain why literature is valuable in the English class. Then I will review the periods and authors most suitable for Primary, followed by the main literary genres and the typology of texts. After that, I will propose didactic applications for each cycle, and I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Why literature in the English classroom?

To begin with, literature offers much more than language practice, although its pedagogical use is developed in depth in topic 16. Firstly, it provides **rich and memorable input**, with repetition, rhythm and predictable patterns. Secondly, it connects with children's **emotions and imagination**, which strengthens memory. Thirdly, it opens a window onto **other cultures**. Finally, it builds **reading habits**: the meta-analysis by **Mol and Bus (2011)** found that frequent reading for pleasure is associated with better comprehension, vocabulary and spelling, in a reciprocal relationship that grows stronger with age. Moreover, **Bland (2013)** argues that **authentic children's literature**, especially picture books, empowers young learners because it offers genuine stories rather than texts created only to teach grammar.

## 3. Periods and authors suitable for Primary

With these benefits in mind, let us now travel through the history of English literature for children, following the periods described by **Hunt (1994)**.

### 3.1. The oral tradition

The oldest literature children receive is **oral**. **Nursery rhymes** such as *Humpty Dumpty*, *Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star* or *Hickory Dickory Dock* were passed down for generations and later collected by folklorists such as **Opie and Opie (1951)**. Their rhythm, rhyme and brevity make them ideal for the first cycle. Likewise, **folk tales** such as *Jack and the Beanstalk* or *The Three Little Pigs* offer simple structures, repeated phrases and clear characters.

### 3.2. Victorian and Edwardian classics

In the 19th century, children's literature became a genre in its own right. **Edward Lear** popularised **nonsense limericks** in his *Book of Nonsense* (1846), and **Lewis Carroll** created *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* (1865), full of wordplay. In the early 20th century, **Beatrix Potter** published *The Tale of Peter Rabbit* (1902), **Rudyard Kipling** wrote the *Just So Stories* (1902), explaining how animals got their features, and **J. M. Barrie** created **Peter Pan**. Although the original texts are too difficult for Primary, adapted versions, films and short extracts can introduce these famous characters.

### 3.3. The 20th century

During the 20th century, several authors created works that still delight children. **A. A. Milne** gave us *Winnie-the-Pooh* (1926). In the United States, **Dr. Seuss** wrote *The Cat in the Hat* (1957) using a very limited vocabulary, which makes it particularly useful for beginners, and **Maurice Sendak** published *Where the Wild Things Are* (1963), a short picture book about emotions. In Britain, **Roald Dahl** wrote humorous and slightly rebellious stories, such as *Charlie and the Chocolate Factory* (1964) and *Matilda* (1988), which are excellent for the third cycle in adapted versions.

### 3.4. Contemporary picture books

Finally, contemporary **picture books** are perhaps the most valuable resource for Primary. **Eric Carle**'s *The Very Hungry Caterpillar* (1969) teaches days, numbers and food; **Michael Rosen**'s *We're Going on a Bear Hunt* (1989) combines rhythm and action; **Anthony Browne**'s books, such as *Gorilla* (1983), invite pupils to interpret pictures; and **Julia Donaldson**'s *The Gruffalo* (1999) uses rhyme, repetition and a clever plot. Furthermore, teachers should include **diverse voices** and authors from different English-speaking countries, so that pupils see a varied picture of the English-speaking world.

## 4. Literary genres

Having reviewed periods and authors, we can now classify literary works into genres, the third element of the title.

Traditionally, literature is divided into three main genres. **Narrative** tells a story with characters, setting and plot, as in tales, fables and novels; with young learners, stories with **repetition and a predictable structure** work best. **Poetry** plays with rhythm, rhyme and sound, as in nursery rhymes, limericks, acrostics and haiku. **Drama** is written to be performed, and short plays, puppet shows and Readers' Theatre scripts are very suitable for Primary. For example, a **limerick** such as Lear's *"There was an Old Man with a beard…"* shows pupils, in only five lines, how rhythm and rhyme create humour, and third-year pupils can then invent a funny last line of their own. Similarly, **fables**, such as *The Tortoise and the Hare*, combine a short narrative with a clear moral, which invites pupils to give their opinion in simple English.

In addition, there are modern formats that combine genres. **Picture books** tell stories through both words and pictures, and **comics and graphic novels** use panels and speech bubbles, which help pupils to follow a story with less text.

When selecting texts, teachers should consider the pupils' **age and interests**, the **language level** (pupils need to know most of the words; see **Nation, 2013**), the presence of **repetition and visual support**, the **cultural value** of the text and its **potential for activities**.

## 5. Typology of texts

However, literature is only one kind of text. The title also asks us to consider the **typology of texts**, which helps pupils to recognise different purposes and structures.

One of the most widely used classifications was proposed by **Werlich (1976)**, who distinguished five text types according to their main function:
- **Descriptive texts** present features of people, places or objects (*My monster has got three eyes*). Example: a description of a pet.
- **Narrative texts** tell events in time order (*Once upon a time… Then… Finally…*). Example: a fairy tale.
- **Expository texts** explain or inform (*Bees make honey*). Example: a short fact file about animals.
- **Argumentative texts** give opinions and reasons (*I think… because…*). Example: a poster on why we should recycle.
- **Instructive texts** tell the reader what to do (*First, cut… Then, mix…*). Example: a recipe or the rules of a game.

In the classroom, recognising these types helps pupils to **read with a purpose** and to **write with a model**. For example, before writing their own recipe, pupils can identify the imperatives, numbers and sequence words in a model recipe.

## 6. Didactic application by cycle

All this knowledge must finally be adapted to each stage of Primary:
- **First cycle**: nursery rhymes, action rhymes and **picture books** with repetition, such as *The Very Hungry Caterpillar*; mainly oral work through storytelling, chants and TPR; simple descriptive texts (*It's a big, red apple*).
- **Second cycle**: **folk tales**, short poems and picture books with more text; shared reading, sequencing activities and simple narrative and instructive texts, such as a recipe or the rules of a game.
- **Third cycle**: **adapted novels**, comics, limericks and short plays; reading circles, comparison of text types and production of descriptive, narrative and argumentative texts.

**Example (6th year): *"Our Roald Dahl week"***. Pupils read an adapted extract of *Charlie and the Chocolate Factory*, describe a new invention for the factory (**descriptive text**), write the instructions to make their own chocolate bar (**instructive text**), and give their opinion of the book in a short review (**argumentative text**). Finally, they perform a short scene for younger pupils.

## 7. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, literature must be accessible to every pupil. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, teachers should offer texts in **different formats**, such as audiobooks, picture books, comics and digital stories, and allow pupils some **choice** in what they read. Pupils with **dyslexia** benefit from audio versions and dyslexia-friendly fonts, newly arrived pupils from wordless picture books, and pupils with high ability from longer or more complex texts.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**. Simple **reading records**, oral retellings, observation of participation in storytelling and **rubrics** for written texts allow teachers to assess both comprehension and pupils' growing ability to use different text types.

## 8. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the bear hunt, which turned a whole class into enthusiastic explorers. As we have seen, English offers an extraordinary literary heritage for children, from **nursery rhymes and folk tales** to **Victorian classics**, **20th-century favourites** and **contemporary picture books**. Knowing these **periods, authors and genres**, as well as the main **text types**, allows teachers to choose the right text for each moment and each child. Our challenge is to make every lesson with a book an adventure, so that, like the children in the story, our pupils always want to go on another hunt, this time for new stories in English.

> - Literature: rich input, emotion, culture, reading habits (**Mol & Bus, 2011**); authentic picture books (**Bland, 2013**).

> - Genres: narrative, poetry, drama; picture books and comics; selection criteria (level: **Nation, 2013**).
