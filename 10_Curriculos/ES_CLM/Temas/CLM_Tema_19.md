## 1. Introduction

Every Monday, a first-year English class receives a visit from **Mr Bean-Bag**, a funny puppet who only understands English. Pupils who are usually silent lean forward to tell him about their weekend: *"I play football!"*, *"My dog!"*. When the puppet pretends not to understand, they repeat, point and mime. Somehow, speaking to a puppet is less frightening than speaking to the teacher. This small example shows how animation and drama techniques can unlock children's expression, which is the subject of this topic.

Indeed, the current curriculum supports these techniques. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha promote active and cooperative methodologies, oral **interaction** in everyday situations, and the use of **non-verbal and paralinguistic resources** to communicate. Moreover, drama connects the foreign language area with artistic education and with personal and social development.

To develop this topic, I will first describe the main animation and expression techniques. Then I will analyse dramatization, both of everyday situations and of stories and characters. After that, I will explain how to organise group work for creative tasks and the role of the teacher. Finally, I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Techniques for animation and expression

To begin with, **animation and expression techniques** are activities that encourage pupils to express themselves with their **voice, body and imagination**, creating a lively and motivating atmosphere. **Maley and Duff (2005)** argue that drama techniques give learners a real reason to use language, involve the whole person and make language memorable. The main techniques for Primary are:
- **Mime**: expressing ideas without words, for example miming an action for classmates to guess (*You're swimming!*).
- **Total Physical Response**, proposed by **Asher (1969)**, in which pupils respond physically to commands, as a first step towards expression.
- **Puppets and masks**, which allow shy pupils to speak "through" a character.
- **Storytelling** with voices and gestures, in which pupils join in with sound effects and refrains.
- **Freeze frames**: groups create a still image of a key moment in a story, and the teacher "brings it to life" by tapping a pupil, who says one sentence.
- **Readers' Theatre**: pupils read a script aloud expressively, without memorising or acting, which develops fluency and intonation.
- **Voice games**: saying the same sentence in different ways (*happy, sad, angry, tired*) to practise intonation and emotions.

For example, the sentence *"I've lost my cat"* said sadly, angrily and as a whisper allows pupils to explore how intonation changes meaning. These techniques have several benefits: they **lower anxiety**, because pupils hide behind a character or a mask; they involve **movement and emotion**, which make language memorable; they develop **non-verbal communication**; and they create a positive group atmosphere in which everyone has a part to play.

## 3. Dramatization of everyday situations and performance of stories and characters

Having seen these basic techniques, let us now move on to dramatization, which the title divides into two areas.

### 3.1. Everyday situations

First, the dramatization of **everyday situations** prepares pupils for real communication. **Role-plays** and **simulations**, such as shopping, ordering food, asking for directions or visiting the doctor, allow pupils to practise functional language in context. With young learners, these situations should be **scaffolded**: role cards with pictures, a model dialogue and props such as toy money or a menu. For instance, in a market role-play, half the class are sellers and the other half buyers, using *"How much is it?" "It's two euros." "Here you are."*. As pupils gain confidence, the teacher can move from **scripted** dialogues to **semi-scripted** ones, in which only some lines are given, and finally to **improvisations**, in which a small problem is introduced, such as *"The shop has no more apples!"*, so that pupils must react spontaneously.

### 3.2. Stories and characters

Second, the **performance of stories and characters** connects drama with literature. Pupils can **act out** a known story, such as *The Three Little Pigs*, after listening to it several times. They can also explore characters through **hot-seating**, in which a pupil plays a character and the class asks questions (*"Why did you blow the house down, Wolf?"*), or through **process drama**, in which the whole class enters an imaginary situation together. In the **mantle of the expert** approach developed by Dorothy Heathcote (**Heathcote & Bolton, 1995**), pupils take on the role of experts, such as zookeepers or detectives, who must solve a problem, which gives a powerful sense of purpose.

### 3.3. Stages of a drama activity

Furthermore, drama activities work best when they follow clear stages, as **Phillips (1999)** suggests: a **warm-up** to relax the body and voice; **preparation**, in which pupils learn the language and plan their scene; the **performance**, for the class or another audience; and **reflection**, in which pupils comment on what went well and what they learned. This structure ensures that drama is not improvisation without aims, but a planned learning experience. For example, before acting out *The Three Little Pigs*, pupils can warm up by walking like pigs and blowing like the wolf, prepare the key lines in groups, perform the story with simple masks and, finally, say which part they enjoyed most and which new words they used.

## 4. Group work for creative tasks

Many drama activities and creative tasks are carried out in groups, which leads us to the third part of the title.

### 4.1. Cooperative learning

However, putting pupils together does not automatically make them cooperate. **Johnson and Johnson (2009)** identified five essential elements of **cooperative learning**: **positive interdependence**, so that the group can only succeed if everyone contributes; **individual accountability**, so that each pupil is responsible for their part; **promotive interaction**, in which pupils help and encourage each other; **social skills**, such as listening and taking turns; and **group processing**, in which the group reflects on how it worked. Their research shows that, when these conditions are met, cooperative learning improves both achievement and social relationships.

### 4.2. Organising groups

In practice, groups of **three to five** pupils are usually best for creative tasks. **Heterogeneous** groups allow pupils to support each other, and each pupil should have a clear **role**, such as director, script writer, props manager or narrator. In addition, the teacher should teach the **language of group work** (*"Whose turn is it?"*, *"I agree"*, *"What about…?"*), so that pupils cooperate in English as much as possible. Teachers should also anticipate common problems, such as one pupil doing all the work or another not participating. Rotating roles, giving each pupil a personal task within the group product and asking groups to evaluate how everyone contributed are simple ways of ensuring **individual accountability**.

**Example (5th year): *"Our class soap opera"***. In groups of four, pupils create a short episode of a soap opera set in a school. Each pupil has a role: the director organises rehearsals, the writer coordinates the script, the designer prepares props and the narrator introduces the scene, while everyone also acts. The groups write their scripts with a model, rehearse, perform for another class and finally assess their group work with a simple checklist.

## 5. The teacher's role

As these examples show, the teacher's role in drama and group work is very different from that of a traditional lecturer. **Harmer (2015)** describes several roles that teachers adopt depending on the activity:
- **Controller**, when giving instructions or modelling language.
- **Organiser**, when setting up groups, explaining rules and managing time.
- **Prompter**, when helping pupils who are stuck without taking over.
- **Participant**, when taking part in a role-play, for example as the shopkeeper.
- **Resource**, when providing language that pupils need.
- **Assessor**, when observing and giving feedback.

In addition, the teacher should **gradually release responsibility** to pupils: at the beginning, the teacher models and guides; later, pupils plan and perform more autonomously. Above all, the teacher must create a **safe and respectful climate**, in which mistakes are accepted and nobody is laughed at, because drama involves a certain exposure. For instance, during the soap opera project, the teacher acts as **organiser** when forming groups, as **resource** when pupils ask for words for their script, as **prompter** during rehearsals and as **assessor** during the performance, changing roles naturally as the task develops.

## 6. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, drama must include every pupil. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, teachers should offer different ways to participate. **Shy pupils** can start with puppets, masks, mime or behind-the-scenes roles before speaking in front of others. Pupils with **autism spectrum disorder** benefit from clear scripts, predictable routines and the option not to perform in public, while pupils with **ADHD** enjoy the movement of drama but need short, well-structured activities. Newly arrived pupils can participate through mime and short, repeated lines.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**. Teachers can use **rubrics** covering communication, pronunciation, expression and cooperation, while **self-assessment** and **peer assessment** (*"two stars and a wish"*) help pupils to reflect. Recording performances also allows pupils to see and evaluate their progress.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to Mr Bean-Bag, the puppet who only understands English. That puppet shows that, when pupils speak **through a character**, they lose their fear and find real reasons to communicate. As we have seen, animation and expression techniques, the dramatization of **everyday situations** and of **stories and characters**, and **cooperative group work** offer rich opportunities for meaningful and creative language use. Our challenge as teachers is to take on the right role at each moment, as **organisers, prompters and guides**, so that every child can step onto the stage of English with confidence.

> - Animation and expression techniques: mime, TPR (**Asher, 1969**), puppets, masks, storytelling, freeze frames, Readers' Theatre, voice games (**Maley & Duff, 2005**).
> - Everyday situations: scaffolded role-plays and simulations · Stories and characters: acting out, hot-seating, process drama, mantle of the expert (**Heathcote & Bolton, 1995**).
> - Stages: warm-up → preparation → performance → reflection (**Phillips, 1999**).
> - Cooperative learning: interdependence, accountability, interaction, social skills, group processing (**Johnson & Johnson, 2009**); groups of 3-5 with roles.
