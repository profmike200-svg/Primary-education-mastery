## 1. Introduction

In the 1950s, a Spanish grandfather learned French by translating sentences such as ***"The pen of my aunt is on the table"*** and memorising verb tables. Seventy years later, his granddaughter is learning English by preparing a short video for a partner school in Ireland. Both have studied a foreign language at school, yet their experiences could hardly be more different. This contrast sums up the **history of language teaching**, the subject of this topic.

Understanding this evolution is not a matter of mere curiosity. On the contrary, every method reflects a particular view of **what language is** and **how it is learned**, and many of today's classroom techniques have their roots in earlier methods. Moreover, the current curriculum, defined by the **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha, is clearly communicative and competence-based, so knowing how we arrived here helps us to apply it with sound judgement.

To analyse this evolution, it is useful to start with the distinction made by **Anthony (1963)** between **approach**, a set of beliefs about language and learning; **method**, an overall plan based on that approach; and **technique**, a specific classroom activity. With this framework, I will review the traditional methods, the humanistic methods, the communicative approach and current approaches, and finally consider what research says, before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Traditional methods

### 2.1. The grammar-translation method

The story begins with the **grammar-translation method**, which dominated language teaching in the 19th century and well into the 20th. Derived from the teaching of Latin and Greek, it aimed to read literature and to train the mind. As **Richards and Rodgers (2014)** describe, lessons consisted of **explicit grammar rules**, bilingual **vocabulary lists** and the **translation** of sentences, with almost no attention to speaking. The mother tongue was the language of instruction, and **accuracy** was the main goal. As a result, many learners could conjugate verbs perfectly but were unable to ask for directions.

### 2.2. The Reform Movement and the direct method

Not surprisingly, at the end of the 19th century, a reaction emerged. The **Reform Movement**, led by phoneticians such as Henry Sweet and Wilhelm Viëtor, defended the **priority of speech** and the study of phonetics (**Howatt & Widdowson, 2004**). In this context, the **direct method**, popularised by the Berlitz schools, banned translation and taught the language **directly through the language**, using objects, pictures and demonstration. For example, the teacher would point to a window and say *"This is a window"* rather than translate it. Although this method was successful in private schools, it required native-like teachers and was difficult to apply in large classes.

### 2.3. The audio-lingual method

Later, during the Second World War, the United States needed to train soldiers quickly in foreign languages, and this led to the **audio-lingual method**. It combined **structural linguistics**, which described language as a system of patterns, with **behaviourism**, which saw learning as **habit formation** through stimulus, response and reinforcement (**Skinner, 1957**). Lessons were based on **dialogues** and repetitive **pattern drills**, such as *"I have a cat → dog → I have a dog"*, and errors were avoided at all costs. However, when **Chomsky (1959)** argued that language is creative and rule-governed rather than a set of habits, the theoretical basis of this method was seriously weakened.

### 2.4. The oral approach and situational language teaching

In parallel, British linguists such as Harold Palmer and A. S. Hornby developed the **oral approach**, later known as **situational language teaching**. Structures were presented orally in **meaningful situations**, for example *"Is it a pen? No, it isn't. It's a pencil"* with real objects, and then practised in a controlled way. Its legacy is the well-known **PPP sequence**: **presentation, practice and production**, which many textbooks still follow. Although PPP has been criticised for assuming that learners produce exactly what they have just been taught, it remains a clear and useful structure for some lessons with young learners.

## 3. Humanistic and alternative methods

In the 1970s, therefore, a number of alternative methods appeared, often called **humanistic** because they paid attention to the learner's emotions and the whole person. **Total Physical Response**, developed by **Asher (1969)**, links language to physical movement: learners respond with their bodies to commands such as *"Stand up and touch your nose"*. The **Silent Way**, proposed by Gattegno, kept the teacher as silent as possible, so that learners would discover the language with coloured rods. **Suggestopedia**, by Lozanov, used music and relaxation to lower anxiety, while **Community Language Learning**, by Curran, treated the teacher as a counsellor who helped learners to translate what they wanted to say. For example, in a Suggestopedia lesson, learners might listen to a long dialogue read aloud over classical music in a relaxed atmosphere. These methods were often criticised for their weak theoretical basis and for being difficult to apply in ordinary schools. Finally, the **Natural Approach**, by **Krashen and Terrell (1983)**, emphasised **comprehensible input** and a low-anxiety environment, allowing a silent period before production.

Although most of these methods are no longer used in their pure form, they left a valuable legacy: **TPR** and **low-anxiety classrooms** are still essential in Primary today.

## 4. The communicative approach

Meanwhile, a more profound change was taking place. Following **Hymes (1972)**, who defined **communicative competence** as knowing what to say, to whom, when and how, language came to be seen as a tool for communication. At the same time, the Council of Europe promoted language learning for European citizens, and **Wilkins (1976)** proposed the **notional-functional syllabus**, organised around meanings and functions, such as *asking for permission*, instead of grammatical structures.

The result was **Communicative Language Teaching (CLT)**, which rests on several principles: **meaning comes first**, activities have **real purposes**, such as **information gaps**, **fluency** is as important as accuracy and errors are seen as a natural part of learning. For example, instead of drilling *"Can I…?"*, pupils use it to borrow materials in a role-play. This approach is the foundation of the **Common European Framework of Reference** and, in turn, of our current curriculum.

This change also transformed the roles in the classroom. In traditional methods, the **teacher** was the authority and the model to be copied, while the **learner** was a passive receiver. In CLT, by contrast, the teacher becomes a **facilitator** who designs situations and supports communication, and learners become **active participants** who interact in pairs and groups. Likewise, the view of **errors** changed: in the audio-lingual method, they were bad habits to be avoided, whereas in communicative approaches they are evidence of learning in progress. For example, a pupil who says *"I goed to the park"* is actually applying a rule, which shows that their grammar is developing.

## 5. Current approaches

Building on the communicative approach, several current approaches have developed. First, **task-based learning** organises teaching around meaningful tasks. **Willis (1996)** proposed a framework with a **pre-task**, a **task cycle** (task, planning and report) and a final **language focus**, and **Ellis (2003)** provided its research basis. For instance, pupils plan a class party and only then analyse the language they needed. With young learners, tasks must be short, concrete and supported by visuals, such as completing a picture or following a map.

Second, **Content and Language Integrated Learning (CLIL)** teaches subjects such as science through English. **Coyle, Hood and Marsh (2010)** describe it through the **4Cs**: **content, communication, cognition and culture**. This approach is the basis of bilingual programmes in Castilla-La Mancha, and studies, such as the review by **Pérez-Cañado (2012)**, show positive effects on language, although the evidence for content learning is still limited. For example, in a third-year science lesson, pupils can learn the parts of a plant by labelling a real plant, while also practising *It has got…* and *It needs water*.

Third, **project-based learning**, **game-based learning** and **digital tools**, including artificial intelligence, offer new ways of creating authentic, motivating contexts, always provided that they serve a clear pedagogical purpose. For example, an AI chatbot can let sixth-year pupils practise ordering food, but the teacher must still plan the task, guide its safe use and check what pupils have learned.

## 6. What research says: beyond methods

With so many methods available, the obvious question is: which one is best? Interestingly, research gives a clear answer: **there is no best method**. **Prabhu (1990)** argued that the success of a method depends on the teacher's sense of plausibility and the context, and **Kumaravadivelu (2006)** went further, proposing a **postmethod condition** in which teachers apply general principles to their own situation, a position often called **principled eclecticism**.

Nevertheless, research does offer useful guidance. The meta-analysis by **Norris and Ortega (2000)** showed that **explicit instruction**, drawing learners' attention to form, is more effective than purely implicit exposure. Therefore, the most effective teaching combines **communication** with **explicit attention to form**. For example, after a communicative game about daily routines, the teacher can briefly highlight the third-person *-s* (*She plays*), and then return to communication.

For the Primary classroom, these findings lead to some clear implications:
- Use English as the main **language of communication**, while allowing the mother tongue strategically when it helps.
- Provide **rich input** through stories, songs and routines, as the Natural Approach suggested.
- Combine **meaningful tasks** with short moments of **explicit attention** to form.
- Keep techniques from earlier methods when they serve a purpose, such as **TPR** for the first cycle or **drills** in the form of games.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the grandfather and his aunt's pen. Language teaching has travelled from **translation and rules**, through **habits and drills** and the **humanistic** focus on the learner, to **communication, tasks and content**. Each stage has left something valuable: the grammar-translation method reminds us that explicit knowledge can help, the audio-lingual method that practice matters, and the humanistic methods that emotions count. Our challenge as teachers is not to follow one method blindly, but to **choose the right technique for each moment and each child**, so that our pupils, unlike their grandparents, leave school able to communicate in English.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Approach → method → technique (**Anthony, 1963**).
> - Grammar-translation: rules, lists, translation, accuracy. Direct method: no translation, oral priority. Audio-lingual: drills, habits (**Skinner, 1957**), criticised by **Chomsky (1959)**.
> - Humanistic: TPR (**Asher, 1969**), Silent Way, Suggestopedia, CLL, Natural Approach (**Krashen & Terrell, 1983**).
> - CLT: communicative competence (**Hymes, 1972**); notional-functional syllabus (**Wilkins, 1976**).
> - Current: TBL (**Willis, 1996**; **Ellis, 2003**), CLIL 4Cs (**Coyle et al., 2010**).
> - No best method (**Prabhu, 1990**); postmethod (**Kumaravadivelu, 2006**); explicit + communicative (**Norris & Ortega, 2000**).
