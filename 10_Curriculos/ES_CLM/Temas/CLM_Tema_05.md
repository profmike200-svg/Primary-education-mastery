## 1. Introduction

In December, a fifth-year class receives a photo from their e-pal in Sydney: a family having a Christmas barbecue **on the beach**, wearing Santa hats and swimsuits. The pupils are astonished: *"Christmas on the beach?"*. That photo leads to a fascinating discussion about the seasons in the southern hemisphere, about the different ways of celebrating the same festival and about why people in Australia speak English. In just one lesson, the pupils have connected **geography, history and culture** with the language they are learning. This is exactly what this topic proposes.

Indeed, language and culture cannot be separated. Words carry the history, customs and values of the people who use them. For this reason, the **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha include **interculturality** among the specific competences of the foreign language area, and the basic knowledge includes the sociocultural aspects of the countries where English is spoken, with an attitude of **respect and curiosity**.

To develop this topic, I will first explain the origin and expansion of English. Then I will describe the geographical, historical and cultural background of the main English-speaking countries. After that, I will propose how to apply these aspects didactically in the classroom, and I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. English in the world

### 2.1. Origin and evolution of the English language

To understand why English is spoken in so many countries, we must first look at its history, which **Baugh and Cable (2013)** divide into several periods. **Old English** (c. 450–1100) began when Germanic tribes, the **Angles, Saxons and Jutes**, settled in Britain in the 5th century; later, the Vikings added many words, such as *sky* and *egg*. In 1066, the **Norman Conquest** brought French to the English court, and **Middle English** (c. 1100–1500) absorbed thousands of French words, such as *beef* and *government*; Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* belongs to this period. **Early Modern English** (c. 1500–1700) was shaped by the **printing press**, the **Great Vowel Shift** and the works of **Shakespeare**. Finally, **Modern English** developed from the 18th century, when dictionaries such as Samuel Johnson's helped to standardise the language.

### 2.2. How English became a global language

From this small island, English spread across the world. According to **Crystal (2003)**, this happened in two stages: first through the **colonial expansion** of the British Empire, which took English to North America, Australia, Africa and Asia; and then through the **economic, scientific and cultural power** of the United States in the 20th century, reinforced by technology and the internet. As a result, as **Kachru (1985)** explained, English is now spoken in an **inner circle** of native-speaking countries, an **outer circle** where it is an official second language, and an **expanding circle** where it is learned as a foreign language, as in Spain.

## 3. Geographical, historical and cultural background of English-speaking countries

Having seen how English spread, let us now take a brief journey through the main countries where it is spoken.

### 3.1. The United Kingdom and Ireland

The **United Kingdom** is formed by **England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland**, and its capital is **London**. It is a **constitutional monarchy** with a long parliamentary tradition. Besides English, other languages such as **Welsh** and **Scottish Gaelic** are spoken. Its culture includes festivals such as **Bonfire Night** on 5 November, which recalls the Gunpowder Plot of 1605, **Pancake Day** and **Boxing Day**, as well as a great tradition of children's literature, from *Alice in Wonderland* to *Harry Potter*.

The **Republic of Ireland**, with its capital in **Dublin**, became independent in the 20th century. **Irish** is its first official language, together with English. Ireland is the origin of **Halloween**, which comes from the Celtic festival of Samhain, and the whole world celebrates its patron saint on **St Patrick's Day**, 17 March.

### 3.2. The United States and Canada

The **United States** is a federal republic of **50 states**, with its capital in **Washington, D.C.** It was originally inhabited by many **Native American peoples**, and later became independent from Britain with the **Declaration of Independence** of **4 July 1776**, now celebrated as Independence Day. It is a country of immigrants, and its cultural diversity is reflected in festivals such as **Thanksgiving**, on the fourth Thursday of November, and in its enormous influence on music, cinema and technology. Moreover, Spanish is widely spoken there, which is an interesting link for our pupils.

**Canada**, whose capital is **Ottawa**, has two official languages, **English and French**, and its indigenous peoples, the **First Nations** and the **Inuit**, are an essential part of its identity. Its national day, **Canada Day**, is celebrated on 1 July.

The comparison between Britain and North America also shows that English itself has **varieties**. British and American English differ in vocabulary (*lift* and *elevator*, *autumn* and *fall*, *biscuit* and *cookie*), in spelling (*colour* and *color*) and in pronunciation. Pupils find these differences fascinating, and discovering them helps them to understand that there is no single "correct" English, but several standard varieties, all equally valid.

### 3.3. Australia and New Zealand

**Australia**, with its capital in **Canberra**, is both a country and a continent, famous for its unique wildlife, such as kangaroos and koalas. Its first inhabitants were **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples**, whose culture is one of the oldest in the world. Since it is in the southern hemisphere, Christmas falls in **summer**, as our introduction showed.

**New Zealand**, with its capital in **Wellington**, recognises **Māori** as an official language, and Māori culture, including the famous **haka**, is a central part of national life.

### 3.4. English beyond the traditional English-speaking world

Finally, a complete view must include countries of the outer circle, where English is an official language alongside local languages. In **India**, English is used in government, education and business, together with Hindi and many other languages, and festivals such as **Diwali** are celebrated by millions. In **Nigeria**, the most populous country in Africa, English allows communication among speakers of hundreds of languages. **South Africa** and **Singapore** are other examples. Including these countries prevents pupils from thinking that English belongs only to the United Kingdom and the United States.

## 4. Didactic application in the foreign language classroom

All this knowledge, however, is only valuable if we can bring it into the Primary classroom in a meaningful way.

### 4.1. Which culture should we teach?

To begin with, it is useful to distinguish between culture with a **capital "C"**, such as history, literature and art, and culture with a **small "c"**, which refers to everyday life: food, homes, school, games and celebrations. With young learners, **small "c" culture** is usually more meaningful, because children can compare it directly with their own lives. For example, comparing a school lunch in the UK with a Spanish *bocadillo* is more engaging than memorising the names of kings.

In addition, culture should be taught from an **intercultural** perspective. **Byram (1997)** emphasised attitudes of **curiosity and openness** and the ability to **interpret and relate** other cultures to one's own, while **Kramsch (1993)** argued that learners develop a "third place" between their own culture and the new one, from which they can understand both. In practice, this means always **comparing**, not only describing.

### 4.2. Principles and resources

Therefore, some principles should guide our teaching:
- **Avoid stereotypes**: not all British people drink tea, and not all Americans live in big cities.
- Show the **diversity** of the English-speaking world, including the outer circle and indigenous cultures.
- Start from the pupils' **own culture** and from their families' cultures.
- Use **authentic materials**: stories, songs, recipes, photos, short videos and maps.
- Use **ICT**: virtual tours of museums and cities, video calls and **eTwinning** projects with schools in other countries.
- Connect culture with the **calendar**: Halloween, Bonfire Night, Thanksgiving, Christmas, Pancake Day or St Patrick's Day.

### 4.3. Example of a learning situation

**Example (5th year): *"A festival calendar for our school"*.** In groups, pupils choose one English-speaking country and investigate one of its festivals: when it is, why it is celebrated, what people eat and do, and what the weather is like at that time of year. They locate the country on a world map, compare the festival with a similar Spanish one, and create a page for a **class calendar** in English. Finally, they present their page to younger pupils and, through eTwinning, share the calendar with a partner school, which corrects any cultural mistakes and adds its own perspective. This situation integrates the four skills, geography and history, and develops **interculturality** and **mediation**.

## 5. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, cultural content must include every pupil. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, information should be presented through maps, photos, videos and real objects, not only through texts. Moreover, pupils from other countries can share their own festivals and traditions, which turns diversity into a source of learning and strengthens their sense of belonging.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**. Teachers can use **rubrics** for cultural projects, an **observation scale** for attitudes of respect and curiosity, and a simple **portfolio** in which pupils record what they have discovered about other cultures.

## 6. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the Christmas barbecue on a beach in Sydney. That photo showed our pupils that English is not the language of a single country, but of **many peoples with different histories, landscapes and traditions**. As we have seen, the history of English explains its global spread, and the diversity of English-speaking countries offers endless opportunities for learning. Our challenge as teachers is to present this diversity **without stereotypes and through comparison**, so that our pupils discover other cultures, understand their own better and become truly **intercultural citizens**.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Old English (Angles, Saxons, Jutes) → Middle English (1066, Chaucer) → Early Modern (printing, Shakespeare) → Modern English (**Baugh & Cable, 2013**).
> - Global spread: British Empire + US power (**Crystal, 2003**); inner, outer and expanding circles (**Kachru, 1985**).
> - UK & Ireland · USA & Canada · Australia & New Zealand · India, Nigeria, South Africa, Singapore.
> - Small "c" culture for young learners; compare, don't only describe; intercultural competence (**Byram, 1997**); "third place" (**Kramsch, 1993**).
> - Avoid stereotypes; authentic materials, ICT and eTwinning.
