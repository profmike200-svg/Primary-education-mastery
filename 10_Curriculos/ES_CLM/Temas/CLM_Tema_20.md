## 1. Introduction

It is the first week of September, and the teachers of a primary school in Cuenca are holding their first staff meeting. The head teacher asks a simple question: *"How are we going to organise English this year? How does it fit with the reading plan, the digital plan and our bilingual programme?"*. The new English teacher realises that her work does not start and end in her classroom: it is part of a **network of documents and decisions** at state, regional, school and classroom level. Understanding this network is the aim of this topic.

Before starting, a note on terminology is necessary. The title refers to the **"curricular project of the school"**, a document from earlier education laws. Under the current law, the **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, which amends the Organic Law 2/2006 on Education, the **curricular concretion** is included within the **educational project** of the school. Therefore, in this topic I will use current terminology while explaining its relationship with the title.

To develop this topic, I will first define the curriculum and its levels of concretion. Then I will analyse the place of the foreign language area in the current curriculum, at state and regional level. After that, I will explain the criteria for developing the school's documents, with special attention to English, before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. The curriculum: concept and levels of concretion

### 2.1. What is the curriculum?

To begin with, the term **curriculum** has been defined in many ways. **Tyler (1949)** proposed one of the first systematic models, organised around four questions: what objectives to pursue, what experiences to offer, how to organise them and how to evaluate them. Later, **Stenhouse (1975)** emphasised that a curriculum is not a closed plan, but a **proposal to be tested in the classroom** by reflective teachers. The LOMLOE combines both views: it defines the curriculum as the set of objectives, competences, contents, methods and assessment criteria of each stage, but it gives schools and teachers **autonomy** to adapt it to their context. In addition, **Jackson (1968)** drew attention to the **hidden curriculum**: the values and attitudes that pupils learn implicitly through school routines and relationships. In the English class, for instance, the way we react to mistakes teaches pupils whether taking risks with a new language is safe or not.

### 2.2. Levels of concretion

Consequently, the curriculum is developed through several **levels of concretion**, from the most general to the most specific:
- **First level: the State.** **Royal Decree 157/2022** establishes the **minimum teaching requirements** of Primary Education, which, according to the LOMLOE, take up **60 % of school hours** in regions without a co-official language, such as Castilla-La Mancha, and 50 % in those with one.
- **Second level: the region.** **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha completes the curriculum, and **Order 185/2022** regulates assessment.
- **Third level: the school.** Each school adapts the curriculum to its context through its **educational project** and the **didactic programmes** of each area.
- **Fourth level: the classroom.** Each teacher designs **classroom programming** and **learning situations** adapted to the specific group of pupils.

For example, the State sets the specific competences of English, the region details the basic knowledge for each cycle, the school decides how English is coordinated with its bilingual programme, and the teacher plans a learning situation about the local market for a specific fourth-year class.

## 3. The foreign language area in the curriculum

Having seen how the curriculum is organised, let us now focus on the foreign language area, the first part of the title.

### 3.1. Elements of the curriculum

Royal Decree 157/2022 and Decree 81/2022 organise the curriculum through several elements. Firstly, the **eight key competences** come from the **Council of the European Union (2018)** Recommendation on key competences for lifelong learning: linguistic communication (CCL), plurilingual (CP), mathematical and STEM, digital (CD), personal, social and learning to learn (CPSAA), citizenship (CC), entrepreneurship (CE) and cultural awareness and expression (CCEC). Secondly, **operational descriptors** describe what pupils should achieve in each key competence by the end of Primary. Thirdly, each area has **specific competences**, linked to these descriptors. Fourthly, **assessment criteria** are established for each **cycle**, and the **basic knowledge** includes the knowledge, skills and attitudes of the area. Finally, all these elements are mobilised through **learning situations**: contextualised activities and tasks that lead to a final product.

### 3.2. The foreign language area

In the foreign language area, the curriculum sets out **six specific competences**: **comprehension**, **production**, **interaction**, **mediation**, **plurilingualism** and **interculturality**. These competences follow the action-oriented model of the **Council of Europe (2020)**, whose *Companion Volume* of the CEFR describes reception, production, interaction and mediation, and includes **Pre-A1 and A1** descriptors that are useful for Primary. In simple terms, pupils **comprehend** oral and written texts, **produce** their own, **interact** with others, **mediate** to help others understand, reflect on how languages work by comparing them (**plurilingualism**), and value cultural and linguistic diversity (**interculturality**). For instance, a pupil who explains in simple English the rules of a Spanish playground game to an eTwinning partner is mediating, interacting and developing interculturality at the same time. The **basic knowledge** is organised into three blocks: **Communication**, **Plurilingualism** and **Interculturality**.

Moreover, the curriculum gives priority to **oral language** in the first cycle, and it emphasises that pupils should use English in **meaningful situations** related to their interests and environment. As **Cameron (2001)** argues, young learners learn best when language is embedded in meaningful activity, which is exactly the spirit of learning situations. For example, a second-year learning situation such as *"Our class pet"* allows pupils to understand a short story, describe an animal and present a poster, while developing comprehension, production and interculturality.

### 3.3. Assessment

Finally, **Order 185/2022** establishes that assessment in Primary must be **continuous, formative and global**, based on the assessment criteria of each cycle, and carried out with **varied instruments**. Results are expressed using qualitative levels, and decisions about promotion are taken at the end of each cycle. In English, this means assessing all the specific competences, not only grammar and vocabulary, using observation scales, rubrics, portfolios and self-assessment. For example, at the end of a learning situation about healthy food, the teacher can combine an observation scale of the oral presentation, a rubric for the written menu and a short *can-do* self-assessment, each linked to a specific assessment criterion of the cycle.

## 4. Criteria for the school's documents

However, the curriculum only becomes reality when each school adapts it to its own context, which brings us to the second part of the title.

### 4.1. The educational project and its curricular concretion

According to article 121 of the Organic Law 2/2006, as amended by the LOMLOE, every school must have an **educational project**, which includes its values, objectives and priorities, and the **concretion of the curriculum**. In Castilla-La Mancha, this project is complemented by the **annual general programme**, which organises each school year, and by the **norms of coexistence, organisation and functioning (NCOF)**, regulated by **Order 121/2022**.

When preparing these documents, teachers should consider several **criteria**:
- **Analysis of the context**: the socio-economic and cultural characteristics of families, the languages spoken by pupils and the resources available.
- **Coherence and coordination**: English must be coordinated vertically between cycles and horizontally with other areas, for example by working on the same text types in Spanish and English.
- **Methodological principles** shared by the whole school, such as active methodologies, cooperative learning and Universal Design for Learning.
- **Assessment criteria and instruments** agreed by the teaching team.
- **Participation**: the involvement of the whole educational community, including families.

### 4.2. Programmes and plans related to English

Furthermore, English is connected to several school plans and programmes:
- **Didactic programmes** of the foreign language area for each cycle or year, which concretise objectives, competences, basic knowledge, learning situations and assessment. A good programme includes an introduction with the context, the sequence of learning situations with their timing, the methodology, the measures of inclusion, the assessment criteria and instruments, and the procedure for reviewing the teaching practice itself (see topic 21).
- **Bilingual and plurilingual programmes**, regulated by specific regional legislation, in which some non-linguistic areas, such as Science or Arts, are taught in English using **CLIL**.
- The **reading plan**, in which English can contribute with a class library, graded readers and storytelling.
- The **digital plan**, which includes the use of digital tools and platforms in English and safe internet use.
- **International projects**, such as **eTwinning** or Erasmus+, which give English a real communicative purpose.

### 4.3. Inclusion

Finally, every school document must guarantee **inclusive education**. **Decree 85/2018** of Castilla-La Mancha regulates the measures of educational inclusion, from **general measures** for the whole school, such as Universal Design for Learning, to **individual measures** for specific pupils. For example, the curricular concretion can establish that all English units include visual support and different ways of showing learning, so that pupils with dyslexia, newly arrived pupils and pupils with high ability can all progress.

## 5. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the staff meeting in Cuenca. The question *"How does English fit?"* has a clear answer: English is part of a **coherent system** that goes from the **State** and the **region** to the **school** and the **classroom**. As we have seen, the current curriculum defines the foreign language area through **key competences**, **six specific competences** and **learning situations**, and the school must adapt it through its **educational project**, its programmes and plans, always guaranteeing **inclusion**. Our challenge as English teachers is to take an active part in this shared project, so that English is not an isolated subject, but a language that connects our pupils with the whole school and with the world.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - "Curricular project" (old term) → curricular concretion within the educational project (LOMLOE; art. 121 LOE).
> - Curriculum: objectives, experiences, organisation, evaluation (**Tyler, 1949**); a proposal tested in class (**Stenhouse, 1975**).
> - Levels: State (RD 157/2022; 60 % of hours in regions without a co-official language) → region (Decree 81/2022, Order 185/2022) → school (educational project) → classroom (programming).
> - Elements: 8 key competences (**Council of the EU, 2018**), operational descriptors, 6 specific competences, criteria by cycle, basic knowledge (communication, plurilingualism, interculturality), learning situations (**Council of Europe, 2020**).
> - School documents: educational project, annual general programme, NCOF (Order 121/2022), didactic programmes, bilingual programmes, reading and digital plans, inclusion (Decree 85/2018).
