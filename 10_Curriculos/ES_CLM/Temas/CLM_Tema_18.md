## 1. Introduction

In a fourth-year class, pairs of pupils are playing *"Guess who?"* with picture cards. *"Has your person got glasses?" "No, he hasn't." "Is it a woman?" "Yes, it is."* After twenty minutes, each pupil has asked and answered around thirty questions in English, using the same structures again and again, and nobody has complained. On the contrary, when the teacher announces the end of the game, the whole class protests: *"One more, please!"*. This scene shows the extraordinary power of games, which is the subject of this topic.

Indeed, play is recognised by the current curriculum. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha promote active, meaningful and motivating methodologies in which pupils use the language in real situations, and they highlight the importance of **creativity** and of a positive classroom climate. In the foreign language area, games offer an ideal context for developing the specific competences of comprehension, production and interaction.

To develop this topic, I will first define play and creativity and explain their functions in language learning. Then I will classify games for language learning and show how they can be used creatively to achieve communicative competence. Finally, I will refer to the management of games, diversity and assessment, before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Play and creativity: concepts

### 2.1. What is play?

To begin with, play is a universal human activity. **Huizinga (1949)**, in his classic *Homo Ludens*, described play as a **free and voluntary** activity, separate from ordinary life, with its own **rules**, limits of time and space, and a sense of tension and joy. This definition highlights two features that are essential in the classroom: games must be **enjoyable** and must have **clear rules**.

### 2.2. Play and child development

Furthermore, psychologists have shown that play changes as children develop. **Piaget (1962)** described three types of play: **practice play** in infancy, based on repeating actions; **symbolic play** in early childhood, in which children pretend (*a box becomes a car*); and **games with rules**, which become dominant from around the age of seven, coinciding with Primary. This explains why Primary pupils love board games, card games and team competitions. In addition, **Vygotsky (1978)** argued that in play children behave beyond their usual level, as if they were "a head taller" than themselves, because play creates a **zone of proximal development**. For example, in a shop role-play, a pupil may produce sentences they would never attempt in a formal exercise.

### 2.3. Creativity

Closely related to play is **creativity**. **Guilford (1950)** distinguished between **convergent thinking**, which looks for a single correct answer, and **divergent thinking**, which generates many possible answers. Divergent thinking is usually described through **fluency** (many ideas), **flexibility** (different types of ideas), **originality** (unusual ideas) and **elaboration** (developing ideas). In the English classroom, creativity appears when pupils invent a new monster, change the ending of a story or create their own game. For example, if the teacher asks *"What can you do with a shoe?"*, a pupil who answers *"Wear it, throw it, use it as a phone, plant a flower in it"* is showing fluency, flexibility and originality, while practising *can* and action verbs. Creativity, therefore, is not reserved for gifted pupils: it can be developed through open tasks in which there is more than one correct answer.

## 3. Functions of games and creativity in language learning

Having defined these concepts, let us now consider why games are so valuable for learning a foreign language. Their main functions are:
- **Motivation**: games create curiosity, challenge and fun, which encourages participation.
- **Meaningful repetition**: as in *"Guess who?"*, pupils repeat structures many times without boredom.
- **Low anxiety**: the focus is on the game, not on the language, so pupils dare to speak.
- **Real use of language**: games give a genuine reason to communicate, such as finding the answer or winning.
- **Retrieval practice**: memory and quiz games make pupils recall words actively, which, according to **Dunlosky et al. (2013)**, is one of the most effective learning techniques.
- **Social skills**: taking turns, cooperating, following rules and accepting defeat.

However, it is important not to exaggerate. A review by **Lillard et al. (2013)** found that the evidence that pretend play *causes* broad developmental benefits is weaker than often claimed. Therefore, games are valuable when they have a **clear learning aim**, not simply because they are games. In other words, a game in which pupils only colour or run, without using English, may be fun, but it is not a language-learning activity. The teacher's task is to make sure that winning the game **requires** using the target language.

## 4. Classification of games for language learning

Since there are so many games, it is useful to classify them, as the title requires. **Wright, Betteridge and Buckby (2006)** classify language games mainly according to their **linguistic aim**, but other criteria are also useful:
- **By language focus**: **vocabulary games** (bingo, *Kim's game*, memory), **grammar games** (*Guess who?*, *Simon says*), **pronunciation games** (minimal-pair bingo, rhyming games) and **spelling games** (hangman, spelling races).
- **By skill**: **listening games** (*Simon says*, *Bingo*), **speaking games** (*Twenty questions*), **reading games** (treasure hunts with written clues) and **writing games** (crosswords, *Consequences*).
- **By type of interaction**: individual, pair, group and whole-class games; **competitive** and **cooperative** games.
- **By amount of movement**: quiet games at the desk and **active games** involving movement, such as running dictations.
- **By stage of the lesson**: **warmers** to start, practice games in the middle and **fillers** or **coolers** to finish.

For instance, a **running dictation**, in which one pupil runs to read a sentence on the wall and dictates it to their partner, is a writing, reading, speaking and listening game, cooperative and active at the same time.

## 5. Games as a creative technique to achieve communicative competence

However, not all games develop communication equally. The final part of the title asks how games can be used **creatively** to develop **communicative competence**.

The most communicative games are those in which pupils must **exchange information** or **create something new**. **Information-gap games**, such as *spot the difference* or completing a map, force pupils to ask and answer. **Guessing games**, such as *Twenty questions*, require questions and deduction. **Role-play games**, such as a market or a restaurant, simulate real situations. **Board games** created by pupils, with question cards in English, combine creativity, reading and speaking. Finally, **creative games**, such as inventing a story with dice that show pictures, develop divergent thinking and fluency.

Games should also be adapted to each **cycle**:
- **First cycle**: action games, TPR games (*Simon says*), songs with movement and simple guessing games with flashcards.
- **Second cycle**: bingo, memory, board games, *Guess who?* and simple information gaps.
- **Third cycle**: quizzes, treasure hunts with written clues, problem-solving games, debates in game format and pupils designing their own games.

**Example (6th year): *"Design your own board game"***. In groups, pupils create a board game about a topic of the unit, such as travel. They write question cards (*"Where would you like to go? Why?"*), design the board and write the rules in English. Then they swap games with another group, play, and give feedback. In this way, they develop reading, writing, speaking and creativity at the same time.

## 6. Managing games in the classroom

Of course, games need careful management to be effective. Firstly, every game must have a **clear linguistic aim**. Secondly, rules should be **explained and demonstrated** in English, with an example round. Thirdly, teachers should choose suitable **grouping** and keep the noise and competition under control, for instance by using cooperative scoring or mixing teams. Finally, there should be **feedback** after the game, noting common errors to work on later. It is also useful to teach pupils the **language of games** from the first cycle (*It's my turn*, *Roll the dice*, *You're cheating!*, *Well done!*), so that the game itself is played in English and not in Spanish.

In addition, **gamification**, the use of game elements such as points, badges and levels in non-game contexts, has become popular. A meta-analysis by **Sailer and Homner (2020)** found small positive effects of gamification on learning and motivation, but these depended on how it was designed. Therefore, points and badges should never replace meaningful tasks; they should support them.

## 7. Attention to diversity and assessment

Games can include every pupil if they are well designed. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, teachers can offer **roles** adapted to each pupil (card holder, scorekeeper, question master), use **visual support** and prefer **cooperative games** when competition causes anxiety. Pupils with **ADHD** benefit from short, active games with clear rules, and pupils with **autism spectrum disorder** from predictable games and advance explanation of the rules. For cooperative games to work, **Johnson and Johnson (2009)** remind us that every member must contribute and be accountable.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**. Games offer excellent opportunities for **observation**: with a simple checklist, teachers can record which pupils use the target structures, how they interact and whether they follow the rules in English.

## 8. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the class that begged for *"One more, please!"*. As we have seen, play is a fundamental human activity that changes with development, and creativity allows children to go beyond what they have been taught. Well-chosen games provide **motivation, meaningful repetition, low anxiety and real communication**, and they can be classified by aim, skill, interaction, movement and stage. Our challenge as teachers is to use games **with a clear purpose**, so that every game becomes a creative path towards communicative competence, and every pupil discovers that learning English can be serious fun.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Play: free, rule-governed, separate from ordinary life (**Huizinga, 1949**); practice, symbolic and rule games (**Piaget, 1962**); play creates a ZPD (**Vygotsky, 1978**).
> - Creativity: divergent thinking: fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration (**Guilford, 1950**).
> - Functions: motivation, repetition, low anxiety, real use, retrieval (**Dunlosky et al., 2013**), social skills; evidence nuance (**Lillard et al., 2013**).
> - Classification by language aim (**Wright et al., 2006**), skill, interaction, movement and lesson stage.
> - Most communicative: information gaps, guessing, role-play, pupil-made board games; gamification has small, design-dependent effects (**Sailer & Homner, 2020**).
