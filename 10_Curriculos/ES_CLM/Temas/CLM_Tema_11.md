## 1. Introduction

Picture a Spanish ten-year-old on holiday, joining a group of English children in a playground. She knows very little grammar, but she manages perfectly with a handful of words and phrases: ***"Can I play?"***, ***"My turn!"***, ***"Cool!"***. In just a few minutes, she is part of the game. This scene illustrates a famous idea by **Wilkins (1972)**: without grammar, very little can be conveyed, but **without vocabulary, nothing can be conveyed**.

Indeed, vocabulary is the basis of both comprehension and production. That is why **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha include lexical content throughout the **Communication block** of basic knowledge, related to pupils' interests, their immediate environment and everyday communicative functions. Furthermore, vocabulary contributes to all the specific competences of the area, from comprehension to mediation.

With this in mind, I will first explain what knowing a word means and how words are learned. Then I will describe the lexical and semantic fields of English and the lexicon required for socialization, information and the expression of attitudes. Finally, I will propose types of vocabulary activities, along with attention to diversity and assessment, before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. General considerations about learning words

### 2.1. What does it mean to know a word?

To begin with, knowing a word is much more than knowing its translation. **Nation (2013)** explains that word knowledge involves three aspects: **form** (how it sounds, how it is written and its parts), **meaning** (the link between form and concept, and its associations) and **use** (its grammar, its **collocations** and when it is appropriate). For example, a pupil who knows *homework* must also learn that we say *do* homework, not *make* homework, and that it has no plural.

It is also important to distinguish between **receptive vocabulary**, the words we recognise when listening or reading, and **productive vocabulary**, the words we can use when speaking or writing. Receptive vocabulary is always larger; for example, a second-year pupil may understand *Put your coat on the hook* long before being able to say *hook*. Therefore, we should not expect pupils to produce every word they understand, but we should gradually move words from the receptive to the productive store.

### 2.2. Which words and how many?

Since time is limited, we must choose words carefully. **Nation (2013)** recommends prioritising **high-frequency words**, which appear in many texts, over rare topic words. He also points out that comfortable reading requires knowing around **98 % of the words** in a text, which means only one unknown word in fifty. Consequently, a short graded reader in which almost every word is known is far more useful than a difficult text full of new vocabulary.

In addition, words are often learned in groups. **Lewis (1993)**, in his **Lexical Approach**, argued that language consists largely of **chunks**, multi-word units such as *Can I…?*, *What's your favourite…?* or *See you tomorrow!*. Children learn these chunks as wholes and use them long before they can analyse their grammar.

### 2.3. How are words remembered?

However, choosing the right words is not enough: pupils also have to remember them. Words are learned through **many meaningful encounters** spread over time. The review by **Dunlosky et al. (2013)** found that **retrieval practice**, actively recalling information, and **spaced practice**, reviewing it over several sessions, are among the most effective learning techniques. For instance, a two-minute memory game with last month's words is more useful than rereading a list.

Words can also be learned in two complementary ways. **Incidental learning** happens when pupils meet words while focusing on meaning, for instance in stories, songs or extensive reading, whereas **intentional learning** happens when they deliberately study words, for instance with flashcards or games. Young learners benefit from both, as long as the incidental encounters are frequent and the intentional work is short and playful.

Finally, **Nation (2013)** proposes balancing four **strands**: **meaning-focused input** (listening and reading), **meaning-focused output** (speaking and writing), **language-focused learning** (explicit attention to words) and **fluency development** (using known words quickly and easily).

## 3. Lexical and semantic fields of English

Having seen how words are learned, let us now look at how they are organised, which is the first part of the title.

### 3.1. Lexical fields

A **lexical field** is a group of words related by form or by topic. On the one hand, **word families** share the same root: *teach, teacher, teaching*. On the other hand, **topic-based sets** group words around a theme, such as food, clothes or animals. Organising vocabulary this way helps pupils to store and recall it, as when they build a mind map of *the house* with rooms and furniture. Topic sets are also the natural organisation of most units in Primary textbooks. Nevertheless, teachers should be aware that presenting very similar words together, such as all the colours or all the numbers in one lesson, can sometimes cause confusion, so it is advisable to mix them with words already known and to contextualise them in a story or a task.

### 3.2. Semantic fields and sense relations

Going one step further, **Trier (1931)** developed **semantic field theory**: words belonging to the same area of meaning divide that area between them and **define each other**. For example, the meaning of *warm* depends on *hot* and *cool*. Within these fields, **Lyons (1977)** described several **sense relations**:
- **Synonymy**: similar meaning (*big / large*).
- **Antonymy**: opposite meaning (*hot / cold*).
- **Hyponymy**: inclusion (*animal* → *dog, cat, horse*).
- **Meronymy**: part-whole relations (*hand* → *finger*).

These relations are very useful in the classroom: pupils can play *opposites* memory games, sort hyponyms into categories or label the parts of a body.

### 3.3. Word formation

Moreover, English builds new words through **affixation**, with prefixes (*un-happy*) and suffixes (*teach-er*), and through **compounding** (*foot + ball*). In the third cycle, discovering these patterns allows pupils to understand new words on their own; for instance, once they know *-er*, they can work out *player*, *singer* and *painter*.

## 4. Lexicon for socialization, information and expression of attitudes

Besides fields and topics, the title asks us to consider vocabulary according to its **communicative function**. This perspective is essential, because pupils need words to *do* things with language.

| Function | Examples of chunks |
|---|---|
| **Socialization** | greetings and farewells, introductions (*This is my friend…*), thanking and apologising, classroom language (*Can I go to the toilet, please?*) |
| **Information** | personal information, time, places and descriptions (*Where's the library? It's next to…*) |
| **Expression of attitudes** | likes and dislikes (*I love / I don't like*), feelings (*I'm tired*), opinions (*I think… because…*), agreement (*Me too!*) |

These expressions should be taught as **functional chunks** from the first cycle and expanded progressively. For example, a first-year pupil says *"I like pizza"*, while a sixth-year pupil can say *"I prefer pasta because it's healthier"*. In this way, the same function grows with the learner. Classroom **routines** are the best place to practise this lexicon every day: greeting the class, asking how pupils feel with a *feelings chart*, checking the weather and the date, or asking for materials (*Can I have the glue, please?*). Because these situations are real and repeated, the chunks soon become automatic, and pupils start using them spontaneously, which is exactly the goal of the curriculum.

## 5. Types of vocabulary activities

Now that we know which words to teach and how they are organised, we can design activities. Following **Thornbury (2002)**, these can be grouped into three stages.

### 5.1. Presentation

First, new words are presented with **meaning before form** and **oral before written** (**Cameron, 2001**). Useful techniques include **realia** (real objects), flashcards, mime, **Total Physical Response**, stories and songs. For instance, the teacher can take fruit out of a bag one by one, letting pupils guess before saying the word.

### 5.2. Practice and retrieval

Next, pupils need to **retrieve** the words many times. Games are ideal here: ***Kim's game*** (*What's missing?*), bingo, guessing games, spelling races or quick quizzes. Importantly, these games should reappear in later units, so that **spaced retrieval** consolidates learning.

### 5.3. Consolidation and use

Finally, words must be used in meaningful tasks: surveys, descriptions, projects, a class **word wall**, personal **picture dictionaries** and extensive reading. In this stage, vocabulary becomes part of real communication. Digital tools can support all three stages: online picture dictionaries with audio offer a correct pronunciation model, and digital flashcard applications organise spaced review automatically, although they should always complement, not replace, meaningful use in class. Moreover, from the third cycle, pupils can learn to use a **learner's dictionary**, which promotes their autonomy as language learners.

**Example (3rd year, food).** The teacher presents food items from a shopping bag (**presentation**). Pupils play *What's missing?* (**retrieval**), carry out a survey about favourite food (**use**) and design a healthy lunchbox (**production**). Two weeks later, a quick picture quiz checks the words again (**spacing**).

## 6. Attention to diversity and assessment

Naturally, every pupil learns words at a different pace. In line with **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, every new word should be supported by an **image, a sound and a gesture**. Pupils with learning difficulties benefit from **fewer words with more encounters**, newly arrived pupils from a bilingual **picture dictionary**, and pupils with high ability from extension work with synonyms and word families.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment must be **continuous and formative**, and it should evaluate **vocabulary in use**, not only memorised lists. Picture-matching tasks, short descriptions, oral games observed with a checklist and ***can-do* self-assessment** (*I can name ten foods*) give a complete picture of each pupil's progress.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the playground. That girl did not need a long list of words, but the **right words**, learned as **chunks** and ready to use. Words are the **bricks of communication**: selecting the most useful ones, organising them into **fields and functions**, and meeting them again and again in **meaningful and playful activities** is what turns a list into real vocabulary. Our challenge as teachers is to give every pupil enough words to join any game, in any playground, in English.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Without vocabulary, nothing can be conveyed (**Wilkins, 1972**).
> - Knowing a word: form, meaning, use; high frequency; 98 % coverage; four strands (**Nation, 2013**).
> - Chunks (**Lewis, 1993**); retrieval and spaced practice (**Dunlosky et al., 2013**).
> - Semantic fields (**Trier, 1931**); synonymy, antonymy, hyponymy, meronymy (**Lyons, 1977**).
> - Functional lexicon: socialization, information, attitudes, taught as chunks.
> - Activities: presentation → practice and retrieval → consolidation and use (**Thornbury, 2002**).
