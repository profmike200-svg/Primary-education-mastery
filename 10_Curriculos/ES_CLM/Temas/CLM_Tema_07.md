## 1. Introduction

Imagine a seven-year-old girl listening to a story in English for the first time. At first, she only hears a stream of sounds. Then she catches two words she knows, *dragon* and *castle*, and the pictures help her to guess what is happening. By the third reading, she is shouting the repeated line *"Who's afraid of the big green dragon?"* before the teacher does. In just a few days, she has travelled from hearing to understanding, and from silence to speaking. This journey is the subject of this topic.

Oral language is the basis of human communication. Children learn to listen and speak long before they learn to read and write, and in the foreign language classroom this order should be respected. Royal Decree 157/2022 and Decreto 81/2022 of Castilla-La Mancha give priority to oral comprehension, production and interaction, especially in the first cycle, and these skills contribute directly to the key competences in linguistic communication and plurilingualism.

In this topic I will first describe the main components and features of oral language. Next, I will analyse the complexity of listening comprehension, from simply hearing to active and selective understanding, and how to teach it. Then I will explain how speaking develops from imitation to free production and how to promote it in the classroom. Finally, I will refer to attention to diversity and assessment before drawing my conclusions.

## 2. Oral language

### 2.1. Components of oral language

Speaking a language involves several components working together:
- **Phonological**: sounds, stress, rhythm and intonation (see topic 9).
- **Lexical**: the words and chunks we need to express meaning.
- **Grammatical**: the structures that organise words into sentences.
- **Pragmatic and discourse**: using language appropriately, taking turns and connecting ideas.
- **Non-verbal**: gestures, facial expression and body language, which are especially important for young learners (see topic 2).

### 2.2. Features of spoken language

Spoken language is very different from written language. Brown and Yule (1983) explain that speech is usually spontaneous, full of repetitions, incomplete sentences and fillers such as *well* or *you know*, and strongly supported by the shared context. These features make conversation natural, but they also make listening difficult for learners, who often expect speech to sound like the dialogues in their textbooks.

## 3. The complexity of global listening comprehension

### 3.1. From hearing to listening

Hearing is a physical process: sound waves reach our ears. **Listening** is an active cognitive process in which we select, interpret and build meaning from what we hear. A child may hear an entire song without listening to a single word, and the teacher's task is precisely to turn hearing into listening.

### 3.2. How we process what we hear

Field (2008) describes two types of processing that good listeners combine:
- **Bottom-up processing**: building meaning from the smallest units, from sounds to words and from words to sentences.
- **Top-down processing**: using knowledge of the topic, the situation and the type of text to predict and interpret the message.

Young learners depend a lot on top-down processing: they use pictures, gestures and the situation to make sense of what they hear (Cameron, 2001). This is why visual support is not an "extra" in Primary, but a basic condition for understanding.

### 3.3. From global to selective understanding

We listen in different ways depending on our purpose:
- **Listening for gist**: understanding the general idea (*Is the story happy or sad?*).
- **Listening for specific information**: catching a particular piece of information (*What colour is the dragon?*).
- **Listening for detail**: understanding the message more completely.

In the first cycle, pupils mainly develop global understanding with strong support. As they progress, they become able to listen selectively and, at the end of Primary, to understand short texts in more detail. This progression is reflected in the CEFR descriptors for the Pre-A1 and A1 levels (Council of Europe, 2020).

### 3.4. Why listening is difficult

Listening in a foreign language is demanding because speech is fast, words are linked together in connected speech, the listener cannot go back as with a written text, and unknown words can cause anxiety. Teachers can reduce these difficulties by choosing short texts with visual support, repeating them several times, pre-teaching key words and, above all, teaching pupils that they do not need to understand every word.

### 3.5. Teaching pupils to listen: metacognition

Vandergrift and Goh (2012) propose a metacognitive approach to listening: learners are taught to **predict** what they will hear, **check** their predictions while listening and **evaluate** their understanding afterwards. Even young pupils can do this in a simple way, for example by looking at the cover of a book and guessing which words they will hear.

## 4. Didactic application of the listening skill

A listening activity is more effective when it follows three stages:
- **Pre-listening**: activate prior knowledge, pre-teach four to six key words with flashcards and give a clear purpose.
- **While-listening**: give tasks that require little production, such as pointing, numbering, colouring, ticking or responding physically. Total Physical Response, in which pupils respond with their bodies to instructions, is very useful in this stage (Asher, 1969).
- **Post-listening**: use the language in a new way by retelling, acting out or personalising the content.

The main source of listening input in Primary is the teacher's own English, together with stories, songs, short dialogues and authentic videos adapted to the pupils' level.

**Example (2nd year).** The teacher tells a story about animals with masks and gestures. During the first listening, each pupil holds up a picture card when they hear "their" animal (selective listening). During the second, the class puts six pictures in order (global understanding). Finally, pupils retell the story in groups using the pictures.

## 5. Speaking: from imitation to free production

### 5.1. The stages of oral production

Oral production develops gradually:
1. **Imitation**: pupils repeat songs, rhymes, chants and short dialogues. Repetition is meaningful when it is linked to actions and situations.
2. **Controlled practice**: pupils use fixed structures in games and drills (*Have you got a…? Yes, I have / No, I haven't*).
3. **Guided production**: pupils produce language with support, such as speaking frames, role-play cards or information-gap tasks.
4. **Free production**: pupils express their own ideas in short presentations, personal conversations or creative tasks.

### 5.2. Accuracy and fluency

Brumfit (1984) distinguishes between **accuracy** (using the language correctly) and **fluency** (using it naturally and without long pauses). Both are necessary, but in Primary fluency activities should not be constantly interrupted by correction; accuracy can be worked on in specific moments.

### 5.3. Why interaction and output matter

Long (1996) argued that interaction helps acquisition, because when learners negotiate meaning (*"Sorry? What do you mean?"*) they receive input adapted to their needs. Swain (1985) added that producing language, or *output*, makes learners notice what they cannot yet say and push their language forward. Both ideas support giving pupils frequent opportunities to speak, not only to listen.

### 5.4. Corrective feedback

A meta-analysis by Lyster and Saito (2010) found that oral corrective feedback has significant and durable effects, that younger learners benefit more from it, and that **prompts**, which lead pupils to correct themselves, are more effective than recasts, in which the teacher simply repeats the correct form. In practice, a prompt can be as simple as raising an eyebrow and repeating the error with rising intonation: *"Yesterday I… go?"*.

## 6. Didactic application of the speaking skill

Some practical principles are:
- Teach **chunks** and classroom language from the very first day (*Can I go to the toilet, please?*).
- Display **speaking frames** on the walls and remove them gradually.
- Create **real reasons to speak** through information gaps, surveys and games.
- Let pupils **rehearse in pairs** before speaking in front of the whole class, which lowers anxiety.
- Use short, regular **routines**, such as the weather or the date, so that every child speaks every day.

**Example (4th year).** Pupils carry out a *"Find someone who…"* survey about pets. Then they report the results (*"Three children have got a dog"*) and create a class graph. Finally, each group presents its graph in a short oral presentation using a frame.

## 7. Attention to diversity and assessment

Following the principles of Universal Design for Learning and Decreto 85/2018, oral activities should offer multiple ways to participate. Pupils with a developmental language disorder benefit from sentence frames, visual support and extra modelling. Pupils with autism spectrum disorder need explicit rules for turn-taking and conversation. Shy or anxious pupils can start by speaking in pairs or recording themselves, and newly arrived pupils may go through a natural silent period in which listening comes before speaking.

According to Orden 185/2022, assessment must be continuous and formative. Listening can be assessed through picture-based tasks, and speaking through observation scales that consider communication, vocabulary, pronunciation and fluency. Short recordings at different moments of the year are a powerful way to show pupils, and families, how much they have progressed.

## 8. Conclusion

Let us go back to the girl and her big green dragon. From the first sounds a child hears to the first sentence she builds on her own, oral language develops step by step. Listening is not passive, but an active search for meaning; speaking is not repetition, but a progressive conquest of autonomy. Our role as teachers is to provide rich, comprehensible input, real reasons to speak and patient, intelligent feedback, so that every child can travel from hearing to understanding and from imitation to free production.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Oral language: 5 components; spoken language is spontaneous and context-dependent (Brown & Yule, 1983).
> - Hearing ≠ listening; bottom-up + top-down processing (Field, 2008); young learners rely on context (Cameron, 2001).
> - Gist → specific information → detail; metacognitive listening (Vandergrift & Goh, 2012).
> - Speaking: imitation → controlled → guided → free; accuracy and fluency (Brumfit, 1984).
> - Interaction (Long, 1996) and output (Swain, 1985); prompts work better than recasts (Lyster & Saito, 2010).
