## 1. Introduction

Imagine a seven-year-old girl listening to a story in English for the first time. At first, she only **hears** a stream of sounds. Then she catches two words she knows, *dragon* and *castle*, and the pictures help her to guess what is happening. By the third reading, she is shouting the repeated line *"Who's afraid of the big green dragon?"* before the teacher does. In just a few days, she has travelled **from hearing to understanding, and from silence to speaking**. This journey is the subject of this topic.

Indeed, oral language is the basis of human communication. Children learn to listen and speak long before they learn to read and write, and the foreign language classroom should respect this natural order. Accordingly, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha give priority to **oral comprehension, production and interaction**, especially in the first cycle, and these skills contribute directly to the key competences in **linguistic communication** and **plurilingualism**.

With this in mind, I will first describe the components and features of oral language. Next, I will analyse the complexity of listening comprehension and how to teach it. Then I will explain how speaking develops from imitation to free production and how to promote it. Finally, I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing my conclusions.

## 2. Oral language

### 2.1. Components of oral language

To begin with, speaking a language involves several components working together:
- **Phonological**: sounds, stress, rhythm and **intonation** (see topic 9). For example, *"You're coming?"* with rising intonation becomes a question.
- **Lexical**: the words and **chunks** we need to express meaning.
- **Grammatical**: the structures that organise words into sentences.
- **Pragmatic and discourse**: using language appropriately, **taking turns** and connecting ideas.
- **Non-verbal**: gestures and facial expression, which are especially important for young learners (see topic 2).

### 2.2. Features of spoken language

Beyond these components, spoken language has its own features. **Brown and Yule (1983)** explain that speech is usually **spontaneous**, full of repetitions, incomplete sentences and **fillers** such as *well* or *you know*, and strongly supported by the **shared context**. For instance, a child pointing at a toy and saying *"That one, please!"* is perfectly understood in the shop, but not on the phone. These features make conversation natural; however, they also make listening difficult for learners, who often expect real speech to sound like textbook dialogues.

## 3. The complexity of global listening comprehension

Precisely because speech is so complex, listening deserves a careful analysis, starting with the first distinction in the title.

### 3.1. From hearing to listening

**Hearing** is a physical process: sound waves reach our ears. **Listening**, on the other hand, is an **active cognitive process** in which we select, interpret and build meaning. A child may hear an entire song without listening to a single word; the teacher's task is precisely to **turn hearing into listening**, for example by asking pupils to stand up every time they hear the word *jump*.

### 3.2. How we process what we hear

But how do we turn sounds into meaning? **Field (2008)** describes two types of processing that good listeners combine:
- **Bottom-up processing**: building meaning from the smallest units, from sounds to words and sentences.
- **Top-down processing**: using knowledge of the topic, the situation and the type of text to predict and interpret.

Young learners depend heavily on top-down processing: as **Cameron (2001)** explains, they use pictures, gestures and the situation to make sense of what they hear. For instance, when the teacher holds up an umbrella and says *"It's raining"*, the object does half the work. This is why **visual support** is not an extra in Primary, but a basic condition for understanding.

### 3.3. From global to selective understanding

Furthermore, we listen in different ways depending on our purpose:
- **Listening for gist**: understanding the general idea (*Is the story happy or sad?*).
- **Listening for specific information**: catching a particular piece of information (*What colour is the dragon?*).
- **Listening for detail**: understanding the message more completely.

In the first cycle, pupils mainly develop **global understanding** with strong support. As they progress, they become able to listen **selectively** and, by the end of Primary, to understand short texts in more detail. This progression is reflected in the **Pre-A1 and A1 descriptors** of the **Council of Europe (2020)**.

### 3.4. Why listening is difficult

Nevertheless, listening in a foreign language remains demanding: speech is fast, words are linked in **connected speech** (*"What do you want?"* sounds like *"Whaddaya want?"*), the listener cannot go back as with a written text, and unknown words can cause anxiety. Teachers can reduce these difficulties by choosing short texts with visual support, repeating them, pre-teaching key words and, above all, teaching pupils that they **do not need to understand every word**.

### 3.5. Teaching pupils to listen: metacognition

Going one step further, **Vandergrift and Goh (2012)** propose a **metacognitive approach**: learners are taught to **predict** what they will hear, **check** their predictions while listening and **evaluate** their understanding afterwards. Even young pupils can do this, for example by looking at the cover of a book and guessing which words they will hear.

## 4. Didactic application of the listening skill

Moving from theory to practice, a listening activity is more effective when it follows three stages:
- **Pre-listening**: activate prior knowledge, pre-teach four to six key words with flashcards and give a clear purpose.
- **While-listening**: tasks with little production, such as pointing, numbering or ticking. **Total Physical Response**, proposed by **Asher (1969)**, in which pupils respond with their bodies to instructions, is very useful at this stage.
- **Post-listening**: use the language in a new way by retelling or acting out the content.

The main source of input is the **teacher's own English**, together with stories, songs and short videos adapted to the pupils' level.

**Example (2nd year).** The teacher tells a story about animals with masks and gestures. During the first listening, each pupil holds up a card when they hear "their" animal (**selective listening**). During the second, the class orders six pictures (**global understanding**). Finally, pupils retell the story in groups.

## 5. Speaking: from imitation to free production

Once pupils have received rich input, the second part of the title comes into play: the path from imitation to free production.

### 5.1. The stages of oral production

Oral production develops gradually:
1. **Imitation**: pupils repeat songs, rhymes and chants, linked to actions and situations.
2. **Controlled practice**: pupils use fixed structures in games and drills (*Have you got a…? Yes, I have*).
3. **Guided production**: pupils produce language with support, such as **speaking frames** or **information-gap** tasks.
4. **Free production**: pupils express their own ideas in presentations, conversations or creative tasks.

### 5.2. Accuracy and fluency

Throughout this process, **Brumfit (1984)** distinguishes between **accuracy**, using the language correctly, and **fluency**, using it naturally and without long pauses. Both are necessary, but in Primary fluency activities should not be constantly interrupted; for example, the teacher can note common errors during a game and work on them afterwards.

### 5.3. Why interaction and output matter

But why is speaking so important for learning? **Long (1996)** argued that **interaction** helps acquisition, because when learners **negotiate meaning** (*"Sorry? What do you mean?"*) they receive input adapted to their needs. Similarly, **Swain (1985)** showed that producing language, or **output**, makes learners notice what they cannot yet say. Both ideas support giving pupils frequent opportunities to speak, not only to listen.

### 5.4. Corrective feedback

Finally, errors are a natural part of this process. The meta-analysis by **Lyster and Saito (2010)** found that oral corrective feedback has durable effects, that **younger learners benefit more**, and that **prompts**, which lead pupils to correct themselves, work better than **recasts**. In practice, a prompt can be as simple as repeating the error with rising intonation: *"Yesterday I… go?"*.

## 6. Didactic application of the speaking skill

Bringing these ideas into the classroom, some practical principles are:
- Teach **chunks** and classroom language from the first day (*Can I go to the toilet, please?*).
- Display **speaking frames** on the walls and remove them gradually.
- Create **real reasons to speak** through information gaps, surveys and games.
- Let pupils **rehearse in pairs** before speaking to the whole class, which lowers anxiety.
- Use short daily **routines**, such as the weather, so that every child speaks every day.

**Example (4th year).** Pupils carry out a ***"Find someone who…"*** survey about pets. Then they report the results (*"Three children have got a dog"*) and create a class graph, which each group presents using a frame.

## 7. Attention to diversity and assessment

Naturally, these activities must be accessible to everyone. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, oral tasks should offer multiple ways to participate. Pupils with a **developmental language disorder** benefit from sentence frames and extra modelling; pupils with **autism spectrum disorder** need explicit turn-taking rules; shy pupils can start in pairs or by recording themselves; and **newly arrived pupils** may go through a natural silent period.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment must be **continuous and formative**. Listening can be assessed through picture-based tasks, and speaking through **observation scales** covering communication, vocabulary, pronunciation and fluency. In addition, short **recordings** at different moments of the year show pupils, and families, how much they have progressed.

## 8. Conclusion

To conclude, let us go back to the girl and her big green dragon. From the first sounds a child hears to the first sentence she builds on her own, oral language develops step by step. **Listening is not passive**, but an active search for meaning; **speaking is not repetition**, but a progressive conquest of autonomy. Our role as teachers is to provide **rich, comprehensible input**, **real reasons to speak** and **patient, intelligent feedback**, so that every child can travel from hearing to understanding and from imitation to free production.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Oral language: 5 components; speech is spontaneous and context-dependent (**Brown & Yule, 1983**).
> - Hearing ≠ listening; bottom-up + top-down (**Field, 2008**); young learners rely on context (**Cameron, 2001**).
> - Gist → specific information → detail; metacognitive listening (**Vandergrift & Goh, 2012**); TPR (**Asher, 1969**).
> - Speaking: imitation → controlled → guided → free; accuracy and fluency (**Brumfit, 1984**).
> - Interaction (**Long, 1996**) and output (**Swain, 1985**); prompts > recasts (**Lyster & Saito, 2010**).
