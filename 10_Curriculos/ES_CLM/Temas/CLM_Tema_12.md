## 1. Introduction

During a weather routine in a third-year class, a pupil looks out of the window and says: ***"Is raining!"***. Later, another pupil introduces herself with ***"I have nine years"***. Both messages are perfectly understandable, but both reveal Spanish grammar hidden inside English words: in Spanish the subject can be omitted (*Está lloviendo*) and age is expressed with *tener*. These everyday errors show why teachers of English need a solid knowledge of English grammar, and of how it differs from Spanish, in order to help pupils communicate accurately.

Indeed, grammar is a central part of communicative competence. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha include among the basic knowledge of the foreign language area the **linguistic units and their meanings**, together with the structures needed to perform basic communicative functions. However, the curriculum does not ask us to teach grammar as an end in itself, but as a **tool for communication**, used progressively in oral and written productions.

To develop this topic, I will first describe the essential morphosyntactic elements of English, comparing them with Spanish. Then I will present the basic communicative structures organised by function. After that, I will explain how grammatical categories can be used progressively in Primary, and how to deal with errors. Finally, I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Essential morphosyntactic elements of English

### 2.1. Word classes

To begin with, English words are traditionally grouped into **word classes**. **Open classes**, which constantly accept new words, include **nouns** (*dog*, *happiness*), **verbs** (*run*, *google*), **adjectives** (*big*) and **adverbs** (*quickly*). **Closed classes**, which rarely change, include **pronouns** (*she*, *it*), **determiners** (*the*, *a*, *my*, *some*), **prepositions** (*in*, *on*, *under*), **conjunctions** (*and*, *but*, *because*) and **auxiliary verbs** (*be*, *do*, *have*, *can*). In Primary, pupils learn most of their vocabulary from open classes, while closed classes, although few, appear in almost every sentence.

### 2.2. Morphology

**Morphology** studies the internal structure of words. On the one hand, **inflectional morphology** adds grammatical information without creating a new word. English has very few inflections: the plural *-s* (*cats*), the possessive *'s* (*Tom's*), the third person *-s* (*plays*), the past *-ed* (*played*), the past participle (*played*, *eaten*), the progressive *-ing* (*playing*), the comparative *-er* (*bigger*) and the superlative *-est* (*biggest*). On the other hand, **derivational morphology** creates new words, as in *teach → teacher* or *happy → unhappy*. Compared with Spanish, which has rich verb endings (*canto, cantas, canta…*), English verbs change very little, so **word order** and auxiliaries carry much of the grammatical information.

### 2.3. Syntax: the sentence

At sentence level, English has a fairly fixed **Subject-Verb-Object** order: *The cat eats fish*. We can distinguish four sentence types: **declarative** (*She likes music*), **interrogative** (*Does she like music?*), **imperative** (*Open the door*) and **exclamative** (*What a beautiful day!*). A key feature of English is **do-support**: in the present and past simple, questions and negatives need the auxiliary *do* (*Do you like…?*, *I don't like…*), which has no equivalent in Spanish.

### 2.4. A contrastive view with Spanish

Consequently, a **contrastive analysis** helps us to anticipate pupils' difficulties, as reference grammars such as **Swan (2016)** show. Spanish pupils often omit the **obligatory subject** (*"Is raining"*), place **adjectives after nouns** (*"a car red"*), forget **do-support** (*"You like pizza?"*), drop the **third person -s** (*"He play"*), translate structures literally (*"I have nine years"*) and use *the* with nouns in a general sense (*"I like the football"*, from *me gusta el fútbol*). Knowing these patterns allows teachers to focus their attention where it is most needed.

## 3. Basic communicative structures

Having described the system, let us now look at grammar from the perspective of communication, which is the second part of the title. In Primary, structures are best organised by the **functions** they perform:

| Function | Basic structures |
|---|---|
| Socialising | *Hello! How are you? I'm fine, thank you. See you!* |
| Personal information | *What's your name? I'm… How old are you? I'm nine.* |
| Likes and dislikes | *Do you like…? Yes, I do / No, I don't. I love… I don't like…* |
| Ability | *Can you swim? I can… I can't…* |
| Possession and description | *I've got… Have you got…? It's big and green.* |
| Location | *There is / There are… It's in / on / under / next to…* |
| Routines | Present simple: *I get up at eight. She goes to school by bus.* |
| Actions in progress | Present continuous: *What are you doing? I'm reading.* |
| Past experiences | *Was / were*, past simple: *I went to the beach.* |
| Plans | *Going to*: *I'm going to visit my grandma.* |

These structures are not taught in isolation, but as **tools for doing things**: asking a classmate about their pets, describing a monster or telling what they did at the weekend.

## 4. Progressive usage of grammatical categories in oral and written productions

But how should grammar be introduced so that pupils can use it progressively? This question brings us to the third part of the title.

### 4.1. Form, meaning and use

First, **Larsen-Freeman (2003)** proposes that grammar has three dimensions: **form** (how it is built), **meaning** (what it means) and **use** (when and why it is used). She even describes grammar as a skill, ***grammaring***, rather than as a list of rules. For example, pupils must know that *going to* is formed with *be + going to + verb*, that it expresses future intentions, and that we use it when talking about plans, such as the class trip.

### 4.2. From chunks to analysis

Second, young children first learn grammar as **unanalysed chunks**. As **Cameron (2001)** explains, expressions such as *Can I have…?* or *I've got…* are initially stored as wholes, and only later do pupils notice their parts and start combining them creatively. Therefore, in the first cycle, grammar should be implicit and embedded in songs, stories and routines.

### 4.3. Focus on form and explicit instruction

Third, communication alone is not enough. **Long (1991)** proposed **focus on form**: drawing learners' attention to grammar **within meaningful communication**, rather than through isolated grammar lessons. Research supports this idea: the meta-analysis by **Norris and Ortega (2000)** found that **explicit instruction** is more effective than implicit exposure, and **Spada and Tomita (2010)** showed that explicit instruction benefits both simple and complex grammatical features. In Primary, explicit attention should be brief and accessible, often through **inductive discovery**, as **Thornbury (1999)** describes: pupils observe examples, find the pattern and then use it. For instance, after a story, pupils sort sentences with *he plays* and *I play* and discover the third person *-s* themselves.

### 4.4. Sequencing by cycle

Consequently, grammar should be sequenced progressively:
- **First cycle**: chunks and formulaic language (*I like*, *I've got*, *It's a…*), imperatives in routines and TPR, mainly oral.
- **Second cycle**: present simple and continuous, *can*, *there is/are*, questions and short answers, with first written productions using frames.
- **Third cycle**: past simple, comparatives and superlatives, *going to*, connectors (*because*, *then*), and simple reflection on rules in oral and written texts.

In all cycles, practice should be **meaningful and playful**. Songs and chants fix structures through rhythm (*Do you like broccoli ice cream?*), guessing games such as *"Is it a…? Have you got a…?"* provide natural repetition, and **substitution tables** help pupils to build their own sentences before writing them. In this way, controlled practice becomes a game, and pupils use the same structure many times without feeling that they are doing grammar exercises.

## 5. Errors and feedback

As pupils use grammar progressively, errors will inevitably appear, and the way we deal with them is crucial.

Following **Corder (1967)**, errors should be seen as **evidence of learning**, because they show the rules pupils are building. **Selinker (1972)** called this developing system **interlanguage**. For example, *"I goed"* shows that a pupil has learned the past *-ed* rule and is overgeneralising it. Regarding correction, the meta-analysis by **Lyster and Saito (2010)** found that oral corrective feedback is effective, especially with younger learners, and that **prompts**, which lead pupils to correct themselves, work better than **recasts**. In practice, teachers should focus on errors related to the structures being taught, avoid interrupting fluency activities and use quick, positive techniques.

**Example (5th year).** During a unit on the weekend, pupils play *"Find someone who… went to the cinema"*. The teacher notes common errors, such as *"Did you went?"*, without interrupting. Afterwards, she writes three anonymous sentences on the board and pupils, in pairs, find and correct the errors. Finally, they write a short text about their weekend, checking their verbs with a colour-coded verb wall.

## 6. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, grammar can be abstract and difficult for some pupils. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, teachers can make grammar **visual and concrete**: colour coding for word classes, sentence-building cards, gestures for tenses and substitution tables. Pupils with a **developmental language disorder** benefit from fewer structures with more repetition, pupils with **dyslexia** from oral practice and visual support, and pupils with high ability from discovering rules on their own.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**, and it should focus on **grammar in use**: observation of oral tasks, short written productions assessed with rubrics, and self-assessment statements, such as *I can talk about what I did last weekend*.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to *"Is raining!"* and *"I have nine years"*. These sentences remind us that grammar is not a list of rules to memorise, but the system that allows pupils to **express meaning accurately**. As we have seen, English morphosyntax has features very different from Spanish, basic structures should be organised by **communicative functions**, and grammar should develop **progressively**, from chunks to explicit reflection, within meaningful communication. Our challenge as teachers is to make grammar **visible, useful and enjoyable**, so that one day our pupils will say, without hesitation, *"It's raining!"* and *"I'm nine years old"*.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - Open vs closed word classes; 8 inflections; derivation; SVO; four sentence types; do-support.
> - Spanish interference: subject omission, adjective order, no do-support, third person *-s*, literal translation (**Swan, 2016**).
> - Basic structures organised by function (socialising, information, likes, ability, location, routines, past, plans).
> - Form, meaning and use; *grammaring* (**Larsen-Freeman, 2003**); chunks first (**Cameron, 2001**); focus on form (**Long, 1991**); explicit instruction works (**Norris & Ortega, 2000**; **Spada & Tomita, 2010**); inductive discovery (**Thornbury, 1999**).
> - Errors as evidence (**Corder, 1967**); interlanguage (**Selinker, 1972**); prompts > recasts (**Lyster & Saito, 2010**).
