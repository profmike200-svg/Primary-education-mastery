## 1. Introduction

One Monday, a mother tells the English teacher that her six-year-old son spent the whole weekend singing *"Head, shoulders, knees and toes"* in the bath, touching each part of his body as he sang. The boy had learned it on Friday and, without any homework, had practised it dozens of times at home. No other activity produces this effect so naturally: songs **stay in children's heads** and travel with them wherever they go. Understanding why this happens and how to use songs well is the subject of this topic.

Indeed, songs have a solid place in the curriculum. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha include **songs, rhymes and chants** among the simple literary texts that pupils should understand and enjoy, and they link the foreign language area with artistic expression and **interculturality**. Moreover, songs contribute to the key competences in linguistic communication, plurilingualism and cultural awareness and expression.

To develop this topic, I will first analyse the song as a poetic vehicle and literary creation. Then I will classify the main types of songs and their selection criteria. After that, I will describe techniques for using songs to learn phonetics, vocabulary and culture, and I will add a note on what research says. Finally, I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. The song as a poetic vehicle and literary creation

### 2.1. Songs as poetry

To begin with, songs are a form of **poetry set to music**. Like poems, they use **rhyme**, **rhythm**, **repetition**, **imagery** and **wordplay**, so they are a clear example of what **Jakobson (1960)** called the **poetic function** of language, in which the form of the message matters in itself. For example, in *"Twinkle, twinkle, little star, / How I wonder what you are"*, rhyme and rhythm make the words beautiful and easy to remember. Many traditional songs are, in fact, part of the **oral literature** of English-speaking countries, passed down from generation to generation.

### 2.2. Songs and memory

But why do songs stay in our heads? **Murphey (1990)** described the **"song-stuck-in-my-head" phenomenon**: after hearing a song, we involuntarily repeat it in our minds, which provides extra, effortless practice. Experimental research supports this idea: **Ludke, Ferreira and Overy (2014)** found that adults who learned phrases in an unfamiliar language by **singing** them remembered them better than those who learned them by speaking. In addition, melody and rhythm divide language into **chunks**, which helps learners to store whole expressions such as *"How are you today?"*.

### 2.3. Songs and emotions

Furthermore, songs create a **positive atmosphere**. They lower anxiety, because pupils sing together and nobody is exposed individually, and they increase **motivation** and group cohesion. For instance, a shy pupil who never speaks alone may sing enthusiastically with the whole class. Consequently, songs are especially valuable in the first cycle, when pupils' productive language is still limited. They are also very useful for **classroom routines**: a short *hello song* to start the lesson, a *tidy-up song* to finish an activity or a *goodbye song* at the end create predictable moments that help children to feel secure and to manage transitions.

## 3. Types of songs

Having seen why songs are so powerful, let us now classify them, as the title requires.

### 3.1. Classification

Several types of songs are useful in Primary:
- **Nursery rhymes**: short traditional rhymes, such as *Humpty Dumpty* or *Baa, Baa, Black Sheep*, with simple rhythm and rhyme.
- **Action songs**: songs accompanied by movements, such as *Head, Shoulders, Knees and Toes* or *If You're Happy and You Know It*, which combine language and physical response.
- **Chants**: rhythmic texts spoken rather than sung. **Graham (1978)**, in her *Jazz Chants*, showed how chants reproduce the **natural rhythm and stress** of spoken English.
- **Traditional and festival songs**: *Jingle Bells*, *Happy Birthday* or Halloween songs, which carry cultural content.
- **Pop songs**: songs by current artists, which motivate older pupils, although their language must be carefully checked.
- **Songs created for teaching**, such as songs about days of the week or the alphabet.
- **Songs created by pupils**: new verses for a known melody or class songs about a project.

### 3.2. Selection criteria

However, not every song is suitable. Following **Murphey (1992)**, teachers should consider several criteria: the **age and interests** of the pupils; the **language** (clear pronunciation, useful vocabulary and structures, and appropriate content); the **length and speed**; the amount of **repetition**; the **potential for actions** and activities; and the **cultural value** of the song. For instance, a song with a fast rap section and complex slang would not be suitable for second-year pupils, whereas a song with a repeated chorus and clear actions would be ideal.

## 4. Techniques for using songs

Once the song has been selected, the key question is how to exploit it. In general, song activities follow three stages: **before listening**, to introduce key words and the topic; **while listening**, with tasks such as ordering pictures, filling gaps or doing actions; and **after listening**, to sing, perform or create. Within this framework, the title highlights three specific aims.

### 4.1. Songs for phonetics

Firstly, songs are an excellent tool for **pronunciation**. Their rhythm reflects the **stress-timed rhythm** of English, in which stressed syllables fall on the beat while unstressed ones are reduced. For example, in *"ROW, ROW, ROW your BOAT"*, pupils naturally stress the content words. Songs also help with **weak forms** and **connected speech**, as in *"What's your name?"* sung as one unit, and with **specific sounds**: a chant full of /h/ sounds, such as *"Happy Harry has a hairy hat"*, helps Spanish pupils to soften their pronunciation of *h*, which they tend to pronounce like the Spanish *jota*. Clapping the beat, marking the stressed syllables and backchaining difficult lines are useful techniques.

### 4.2. Songs for vocabulary

Secondly, songs help pupils to **learn and recycle vocabulary**. The teacher can present the key words with **flashcards** before listening, and then pupils show the cards or do the actions when they hear them, following **Total Physical Response**, proposed by **Asher (1969)**. Other techniques include **gap-filling** the lyrics, ordering lines, spotting the "wrong" word, and **creating new verses** by changing words, for example *"Old MacDonald had a zoo"* with wild animals instead of farm animals. Since songs are repeated many times, they offer the frequent encounters that vocabulary learning needs. In addition, songs can be used for **spaced review**: singing a song from a previous unit for two minutes at the start of a lesson is an enjoyable way of retrieving words that pupils might otherwise forget.

### 4.3. Songs for culture

Thirdly, songs open a window onto **culture**. **Festival songs** introduce traditions such as Christmas carols, Halloween songs or *Happy Birthday*; **traditional songs** can reveal history, such as the old rhyme *London Bridge Is Falling Down*; and songs from different English-speaking countries show the variety of accents and musical styles, from Irish folk to Australian children's songs. For example, third-cycle pupils can compare a Christmas carol with a Spanish *villancico*, discussing similarities and differences in the way both cultures celebrate.

**Example (4th year): *"Our class song"***. Pupils learn a simple song about the weather, do the actions and fill in the missing words (**vocabulary**). Then they clap the rhythm and mark the stressed words (**phonetics**). Next, they discover a traditional British weather rhyme, *"Rain, rain, go away"*, and talk about why people in Britain talk so much about the weather (**culture**). Finally, in groups, they write a new verse about the weather in their town and perform the class song for another class.

## 5. A note on evidence

At this point, it is important to distinguish between well-founded and unfounded claims. Songs are clearly useful for **language learning**, because they provide repetition, chunks, rhythm and motivation. However, it is a myth that listening to classical music makes children more intelligent. The so-called **"Mozart effect"** was based on a small, temporary effect on a spatial task, and a meta-analysis by **Pietschnig, Voracek and Formann (2010)** found little evidence for it. Similarly, the multilevel meta-analysis by **Sala and Gobet (2020)** found that, in well-controlled studies, **music training** does not significantly improve children's general cognitive or academic skills. Therefore, we should use songs for what they really offer: **enjoyable, memorable language practice** and cultural enrichment.

## 6. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, songs must include every pupil. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, teachers can support songs with **gestures, pictures and lyrics on screen**. Pupils with **hearing loss** can follow visual lyrics and actions, pupils with **ADHD** benefit from the movement involved in action songs, and pupils with **autism spectrum disorder** may need warnings before loud music and the option of a quieter role, such as showing the cards.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**. Through an **observation scale**, teachers can record participation, comprehension of key words and pronunciation of rhythm and stress, while short **performances** or recordings show pupils their progress.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the six-year-old who sang *"Head, shoulders, knees and toes"* all weekend in the bath. That boy shows better than any theory why songs are so valuable: they turn language into something pupils **want to repeat**. As we have seen, songs are a **poetic vehicle** that combines rhyme, rhythm and emotion; there are many **types** to choose from; and, with the right techniques, they help pupils to learn **phonetics, vocabulary and culture**. Our challenge as teachers is to choose songs wisely and use them with clear aims, so that English becomes the soundtrack that our pupils carry home.
