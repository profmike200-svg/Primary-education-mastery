## 1. Introduction

It is the end of March, and a group of fifth-year pupils is preparing a birthday card for a pupil in their partner school in Wales. They discuss what to write, choose between *"Happy birthday!"* and *"Have a great day!"*, add a drawing and record a short video singing the song. Without realising it, they are taking decisions about the **sender**, the **receiver**, the **purpose** and the **context** of their message, and choosing between **spoken and written language**. In other words, they are experiencing everything this topic is about.

Indeed, communication is the reason why we teach a foreign language. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)** and **Royal Decree 157/2022** define the foreign language area through **communicative specific competences**, and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha organises them into comprehension, production, interaction, mediation, plurilingualism and interculturality. These competences contribute directly to the key competences in **linguistic communication (CCL)** and **plurilingualism (CP)**. Therefore, understanding how language works as communication is the foundation on which all our teaching is built.

To develop this topic, I will first analyse language as a means of communication. Then I will compare spoken and written language and their implications for the classroom. After that, I will study the functions of language and the factors that define a linguistic situation. Finally, I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Language as communication

### 2.1. Defining language and communication

To begin with, we need to define our key terms. Many definitions of **language** have been proposed. A classic one by **Bloch and Trager (1942)** describes it as **a system of arbitrary vocal symbols by means of which a social group cooperates**. This definition highlights three important ideas: language is a **system**, its symbols are **arbitrary** (there is no natural link between the word *dog* and the animal), and it has a **social purpose**. More recently, **Crystal (2010)** has emphasised that language is used not only to exchange information, but also to express identity, emotions and relationships.

**Communication**, in turn, is the process by which information, ideas and feelings are transmitted from one person to another. Language is the most powerful means of communication, but it is not the only one: gestures, images, music and facial expressions also communicate. For example, a pupil who shrugs their shoulders when asked a question is communicating *"I don't know"* without saying a word.

### 2.2. Human language and other communication systems

But what makes human language special? **Hockett (1960)** identified a set of **design features** that distinguish human language from animal communication. Among them, three are particularly relevant for teachers:
- **Productivity**: with a limited number of words and rules, we can create an unlimited number of new sentences. For example, a pupil who knows *I like* and ten foods can produce many different sentences.
- **Displacement**: we can talk about things that are not present, such as the past, the future or imaginary worlds, which is why children love stories about dragons.
- **Cultural transmission**: language is learned from the community, not inherited genetically, which is precisely why it can be taught.

Consequently, learning a language is not memorising a list of words, but acquiring a **creative system** that allows pupils to express their own ideas.

## 3. Spoken language and written language

Having seen what language is, let us now consider its two main forms, which the title asks us to compare.

### 3.1. The pre-eminence of spoken language

There are several reasons why spoken language is considered primary. Firstly, there is a **historical** reason: humans spoke for tens of thousands of years before writing was invented, and many languages in the world today still have no written form. Secondly, there is a **developmental** reason: every child learns to listen and speak before learning to read and write. Thirdly, there is a **curricular** reason: Royal Decree 157/2022 and Decree 81/2022 give priority to oral comprehension and interaction, especially in the first cycle of Primary.

### 3.2. Differences between spoken and written language

Although both forms share the same system, they differ in many ways. As **Crystal (2010)** explains, spoken language is usually **spontaneous**, **dynamic** and **context-dependent**, with repetitions, incomplete sentences and fillers such as *um* or *you know*, and it relies on **intonation**, gestures and immediate feedback. Written language, on the other hand, is usually **planned**, **permanent** and more **explicit**, with more complex sentences and the support of punctuation and layout instead of voice.

| Spoken language | Written language |
|---|---|
| Spontaneous, time-bound | Planned, permanent |
| Shared context, immediate feedback | Reader absent, no immediate feedback |
| Intonation, stress, gestures | Punctuation, layout, spelling |
| Simpler, looser structures | More complex, compact structures |

Nevertheless, the boundary between them is not always clear: a text message is written but informal and spontaneous, whereas a speech is oral but carefully planned.

### 3.3. Implications for the classroom

As a result, the foreign language classroom should follow the natural order: **oral first, written as support**. As **Cameron (2001)** points out, pupils should read and write words they already know orally. For example, pupils first sing a song about the days of the week and only later match the written words to pictures. In addition, the differences between both forms should be taught explicitly in the third cycle, for instance by comparing an informal chat with a short email.

## 4. Functionality of language

Beyond its forms, language is defined by what we do with it, which takes us to the third element of the title: **functionality**.

### 4.1. Jakobson's functions of language

**Jakobson (1960)** proposed one of the most influential models. He identified six **factors** in any act of communication and linked each one to a **function**:

| Factor | Function | Classroom example |
|---|---|---|
| Sender | **Emotive** (expressive) | *"I'm so happy!"* |
| Receiver | **Conative** (appellative) | *"Open your books, please."* |
| Context | **Referential** (informative) | *"Paris is the capital of France."* |
| Contact (channel) | **Phatic** | *"Hello! Can you hear me?"* |
| Code | **Metalingual** | *"What does 'huge' mean?"* |
| Message | **Poetic** | rhymes, tongue twisters, poems |

In practice, a single message often fulfils several functions at once, although one usually dominates. In the classroom, all these functions should be present: the **phatic** function in greetings and routines, the **metalingual** function when pupils ask about words, and the **poetic** function in songs and rhymes.

### 4.2. Halliday's functions in child language

While Jakobson described adult communication, **Halliday (1975)** studied how children use language and identified seven functions: **instrumental** (*I want…*), **regulatory** (*Do as I tell you*), **interactional** (*me and you*), **personal** (*here I come*), **heuristic** (*tell me why*), **imaginative** (*let's pretend*) and **representational** or informative (*I've got something to tell you*). This model is especially useful in Primary, because it reminds us that children use language mainly to obtain things, play, interact and discover. For example, a role-play in a toy shop activates the instrumental function, and a "let's pretend" story activates the imaginative one.

## 5. Factors defining a linguistic situation

Finally, the title asks us to analyse the factors that define a linguistic situation, which brings together everything we have seen so far.

### 5.1. The elements of communication

The classic model of communication, based on **Shannon and Weaver (1949)**, describes a **sender** who encodes a **message** and transmits it through a **channel** to a **receiver**, who decodes it using a shared **code**. Any element that disturbs this process is called **noise**. For example, when a pupil says *"I'm boring"* instead of *"I'm bored"*, there is a problem with the code, and the receiver may misunderstand the message.

### 5.2. Context and communicative competence

However, this model is too mechanical to explain real communication, because it ignores the **context**. **Hymes (1974)** proposed the **SPEAKING model** to describe all the factors of a communicative event: **S**etting and scene, **P**articipants, **E**nds (purposes), **A**ct sequence, **K**ey (tone), **I**nstrumentalities (channels), **N**orms and **G**enre. Thanks to this model, we understand that the same message changes depending on who speaks, to whom, where and why. For instance, *"Give me that!"* may be acceptable between siblings at home, but *"Could you pass me that, please?"* is expected when speaking to a teacher. This awareness is part of **sociolinguistic competence**, one of the components of communicative competence (see topic 3).

### 5.3. Creating real linguistic situations in the classroom

Therefore, teachers should design activities in which all these factors are **real**: a real receiver, a real purpose and a real context. Some examples are writing a letter to a partner school through **eTwinning**, interviewing a visitor, preparing instructions for younger pupils or recording a podcast for families. When the receiver, the purpose and the context are real, pupils pay attention not only to accuracy, but also to appropriacy.

## 6. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, every pupil communicates in different ways. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, teachers should accept and encourage **multimodal communication**: speaking, writing, drawing, gesturing or using pictograms. For example, a pupil with autism spectrum disorder may benefit from visual supports that make explicit who speaks, when and how, and a newly arrived pupil can communicate first through gestures and pictures.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**. Teachers can observe how pupils adapt their messages to the receiver and the purpose using an **observation scale**, and pupils can reflect on their own communication through simple ***can-do* statements**, such as *I can greet people and introduce myself*.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the birthday card for the partner school in Wales. That simple activity contained a **sender** and a **receiver**, a **purpose**, a **context**, a choice between **spoken and written** language and several **functions** of language at once. As we have seen, language is not a set of rules, but a **creative system for communicating** with others, shaped by the situation in which it is used. Our challenge as teachers is to create **real communicative situations** every day, so that our pupils discover that English is not just a school subject, but a way of reaching people all over the world.
