## 1. Introduction

In one school, a fourth-year class connects by video call with a classroom in Edinburgh. The pupils have prepared questions about Scottish food and festivals, and they listen carefully to the answers, take notes and later write a short report. In another school, the English teacher plays a long cartoon on the screen every Friday afternoon "to practise listening", while most pupils simply watch the pictures. Both classes are using technology, but only in the first is technology **serving a clear learning purpose**. This contrast captures the central idea of this topic.

Indeed, technology has become part of everyday life and of the curriculum. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha include **digital competence** among the key competences, and the foreign language area promotes the use of digital tools to understand, produce and interact in English, and to communicate with speakers from other countries. Moreover, schools must have a **digital plan**, and teachers are expected to develop their own digital competence.

To develop this topic, I will first analyse the technological and pedagogical aspects of audiovisual materials, including the main frameworks and what research says. Then I will describe the use of audiovisual materials in the English class and the computer as an auxiliary resource. After that, I will refer to the digital competence of pupils and teachers, and finally to diversity and assessment, before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. Technological and pedagogical aspects

### 2.1. Pedagogical frameworks

To begin with, using technology well requires more than technical skill. **Mishra and Koehler (2006)** proposed the **TPACK** framework, which states that effective teaching with technology requires the integration of three types of knowledge: **technological knowledge** (how tools work), **pedagogical knowledge** (how pupils learn) and **content knowledge** (what is being taught). For example, knowing how to use a recording app (technological) is not enough: the teacher must also know how pronunciation is learned (pedagogical) and which sounds are important in English (content).

Another popular model is **SAMR**, proposed by Puentedura, which describes four levels of technology use: **substitution** (a digital worksheet instead of a paper one), **augmentation** (the worksheet gives instant feedback), **modification** (pupils record and comment on each other's oral presentations) and **redefinition** (pupils collaborate with a school abroad to create a shared digital book). Although SAMR has little empirical research behind it, it helps teachers to reflect on whether technology really transforms learning.

### 2.2. How we learn from audiovisual materials

Furthermore, cognitive psychology explains why audiovisual materials can be so effective. **Paivio (1986)** proposed the **dual coding theory**: information that is processed both verbally and visually is remembered better. Building on this idea, **Mayer (2009)** formulated principles of **multimedia learning**, such as presenting words together with relevant pictures, avoiding unnecessary decorative elements and not overloading learners with too much information at once. For instance, a short video in which each new word appears with a clear image is more effective than one full of distracting animations and music.

### 2.3. What the evidence says

However, technology does not improve learning automatically. A large report by the **OECD (2015)**, based on PISA data, found that countries which had invested heavily in computers in schools did not improve their results in reading, mathematics or science, and that **moderate use** of computers was associated with better results than either no use or very intensive use. In addition, **Kirschner and De Bruyckere (2017)** showed that the idea of **"digital natives"**, children who supposedly know how to learn with technology simply because they grew up with it, is a **myth**: children may be skilled at using devices for entertainment, but they still need to be taught how to search, evaluate information and learn with technology. Consequently, technology should **amplify good teaching**, not replace it.

## 3. Audiovisual materials in the English class

Having established these principles, let us now look at audiovisual materials, the first element of the title.

### 3.1. Types and uses

Audiovisual materials include **visual** resources (flashcards, posters, photos, interactive whiteboard images), **audio** resources (songs, stories, podcasts, recordings) and **audiovisual** resources (videos, cartoons, short films, video calls). They are especially valuable in the foreign language classroom because they provide **authentic input**, **context** for understanding, **models of pronunciation** and **cultural content**. As **Cameron (2001)** explains, young learners rely heavily on visual and situational support to understand, so images and videos are a basic condition for comprehension in Primary.

### 3.2. Subtitles and captions

In addition, subtitles can support learning. A meta-analysis by **Montero Perez, Van Den Noortgate and Desmet (2013)** found that **captioned video**, with subtitles in the same language as the audio, improves listening comprehension and vocabulary learning in a second language. Therefore, from the second cycle, short English videos with English subtitles can be a useful resource, especially when pupils watch them more than once.

### 3.3. A methodology for using video

However, videos must be used actively, following three stages. **Before viewing**, the teacher activates prior knowledge, pre-teaches key words and gives a clear task. **While viewing**, pupils complete a task, such as ticking pictures, answering simple questions or ordering scenes; the teacher may pause the video, play it without sound so pupils predict the dialogue, or play the sound without pictures so they imagine the scene. **After viewing**, pupils use the language in a new way, for example by acting out or retelling the scene. Videos should also be **short**, usually only a few minutes with young learners.

**Example (3rd year).** Pupils watch a two-minute video about a day at a British school. First, they predict what they will see from a still image. During the first viewing, they tick the activities they see; during the second, with English subtitles, they complete a timetable. Finally, they record a short video about a day at their own school to share with their eTwinning partners.

## 4. The computer as an auxiliary resource for learning foreign languages

Beyond audiovisual materials, the title highlights the computer, which today includes tablets, interactive whiteboards and other digital devices.

The computer can perform several **functions** in the English class:
- **Input**: digital stories, e-books with audio, songs and children's websites in English.
- **Practice**: interactive activities, vocabulary apps and online games with immediate feedback.
- **Creation**: digital posters, comics, presentations, audio recordings and short videos made by pupils.
- **Communication**: email exchanges, video calls and collaborative projects through platforms such as **eTwinning**.
- **Assessment**: online quizzes, recordings to track progress and digital portfolios.

In recent years, **artificial intelligence** tools have added new possibilities, such as chatbots for conversation practice, automatic feedback on pronunciation or the creation of personalised materials by the teacher. However, these tools must be used with **critical judgement**: teachers must check the accuracy of the content, respect the age restrictions of each tool and protect pupils' personal data. For example, the teacher can use an AI tool to create a simple story adapted to the class's vocabulary, review it carefully and then use it as a reading text.

The key principle is that the computer is an **auxiliary resource**: it supports the teacher and the methodology, but it does not replace human interaction, which is essential for learning a language. In practice, this means planning **when** devices are used and when they are switched off: a lesson might start with a song on the interactive whiteboard, continue with pair work without screens, and end with five minutes of recording on tablets, so that technology appears only where it adds real value.

## 5. Digital competence of pupils and teachers

Finally, using technology in class also means developing digital competence. For pupils, the European framework **DigComp 2.2**, described by **Vuorikari, Kluzer and Punie (2022)**, identifies five areas: **information and data literacy**, **communication and collaboration**, **digital content creation**, **safety** and **problem solving**. In the English class, pupils can learn to search for information on child-friendly websites, collaborate on shared documents, create digital content and, above all, use the internet **safely** and **responsibly**, protecting their privacy and respecting others.

For teachers, the **DigCompEdu** framework, developed by **Redecker (2017)**, describes the digital competence of educators, including the selection and creation of digital resources, teaching and learning with technology, digital assessment and the empowerment of learners. In Spain, this framework is the basis of the reference framework for teachers' digital competence, which schools take into account in their **digital plan**.

## 6. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, technology can be a powerful tool for inclusion. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, digital tools can offer **accessibility features**: text-to-speech for pupils with dyslexia, subtitles for pupils with hearing loss, enlarged text for pupils with visual impairments and visual timetables for pupils with autism spectrum disorder. At the same time, teachers must avoid a **digital divide**, making sure that pupils without devices or internet at home are not disadvantaged.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment must be **continuous and formative**. A **digital portfolio** with recordings, videos and written work allows pupils to see their own progress throughout the year, and online quizzes can provide immediate feedback, always combined with the teacher's observation.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the video call with Edinburgh and the long Friday cartoon. As we have seen, technology is only valuable when it is used with a **clear pedagogical purpose**, combining technological, pedagogical and content knowledge. Research shows that **moderate, well-planned use** works better than intensive use, that audiovisual materials help when they follow multimedia principles, and that children are not "digital natives" who learn by themselves. Our challenge as teachers is to use audiovisual materials and computers as **auxiliary resources** that bring the English-speaking world into our classrooms, help every pupil to learn and, above all, **connect people**, just like that video call with Scotland.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - TPACK: technology + pedagogy + content (**Mishra & Koehler, 2006**); SAMR: substitution, augmentation, modification, redefinition (Puentedura).
> - Dual coding (**Paivio, 1986**); multimedia principles (**Mayer, 2009**).
> - Moderate, purposeful use beats intensive use (**OECD, 2015**); digital natives are a myth (**Kirschner & De Bruyckere, 2017**).
> - Captioned video improves listening and vocabulary (**Montero Perez et al., 2013**); before / while / after viewing; short videos.
> - Computer functions: input, practice, creation, communication, assessment; AI with critical judgement and data protection.
> - DigComp 2.2 (**Vuorikari et al., 2022**); DigCompEdu (**Redecker, 2017**); accessibility and digital divide.
