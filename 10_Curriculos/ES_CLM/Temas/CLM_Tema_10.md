## 1. Introduction

A third-year pupil proudly hands in a short text: *"I have a frend. His name is Mark. He is very funy"*. Every word is written exactly as it sounds, which is perfectly logical for a Spanish child, because in Spanish we usually write what we hear. In English, however, *friend* has an *i* that is not pronounced, and *funny* needs a double *n*. This small text reveals the central challenge of this topic: English spelling does not always reflect pronunciation, and our pupils need to understand how it works.

Indeed, the relationship between sound and spelling is essential for reading and writing. The **Organic Law 3/2020 (LOMLOE)**, **Royal Decree 157/2022** and **Decree 81/2022** of Castilla-La Mancha include among the basic knowledge of the foreign language area the **sound-spelling conventions** and basic orthographic patterns, linked to the specific competences of written comprehension and production. Furthermore, a comparative study by **Seymour, Aro and Erskine (2003)** showed that children learning to read in English need considerably more time to master basic decoding than children learning in transparent languages, such as Spanish, which confirms the need for explicit teaching.

To develop this topic, I will first describe the English orthographic code and the relationship between sound and spelling. Then I will analyse the main proposals for teaching the written code and what the evidence says about them. After that, I will explain orthographic applications in pupils' written productions, and finally I will refer to diversity and assessment before drawing some conclusions.

## 2. The English orthographic code

### 2.1. An opaque orthography

To begin with, English uses an alphabet of **26 letters** to represent around **44 phonemes**, so many sounds must be represented by combinations of letters. In addition, English is an **opaque orthography**: the same sound can be spelled in different ways, and the same spelling can represent different sounds.

But why is English spelling so irregular? According to **Baugh and Cable (2013)**, there are several historical reasons. Firstly, spelling began to be fixed with the arrival of the **printing press** in England in the late 15th century, just while the **Great Vowel Shift** was changing the pronunciation of long vowels. As a result, words such as *name* or *time* kept spellings that reflect an older pronunciation. Secondly, English has absorbed thousands of **borrowings** with their original spellings, such as *ph* in *photo* from Greek or *ch* in *chef* from French. Thirdly, some Renaissance scholars added **etymological letters** to show a word's Latin origin, such as the *b* in *debt*. Finally, some letters stopped being pronounced, like the *k* in *knight*, which was once sounded.

### 2.2. Spelling patterns

Nevertheless, English spelling is far more **regular** than it seems, and teachers can show pupils its main patterns:
- **Consonant digraphs**: two letters, one sound, such as *sh* (*ship*), *ch* (*chair*), *th* (*think*), *ph* (*phone*) and *ck* (*duck*).
- **Vowel digraphs**: *ee* (*tree*), *ea* (*sea*), *oa* (*boat*), *ai* (*rain*).
- **The split digraph** or "magic *e*": a final *e* changes the vowel sound, as in *cap/cape*, *kit/kite* or *hop/hope*.
- **Silent letters**: *knee*, *write*, *lamb*.
- **Consonant doubling** after short vowels: *hop → hopping*, *run → running*.
- **Morphological spelling**: English often keeps the same spelling for a morpheme even when its pronunciation changes. For example, the past ending *-ed* is pronounced /t/, /d/ or /ɪd/ (*worked*, *played*, *wanted*), but it is always written *-ed*, which makes words easier to recognise.

### 2.3. British and American spelling

Moreover, English has two main spelling standards. American spelling was partly simplified by **Webster (1828)** in his dictionary, which explains differences such as *colour/color*, *centre/center*, *travelled/traveled* and *organise/organize*. Teachers should choose one standard for consistency, but accept both and help pupils to recognise them.

## 3. Sound and orthography

Having described the code, let us now consider more closely the relationship between sound and spelling, the second element of the title.

On the one hand, **one sound can have many spellings**. For example, the sound /iː/ can be written *ee* (*see*), *ea* (*sea*), *ie* (*field*), *e* (*me*) or *ey* (*key*). On the other hand, **one spelling can represent several sounds**: *ea* sounds different in *bread*, *bead* and *great*. Consequently, pupils cannot rely on the simple Spanish rule "write what you hear".

However, presenting English as chaotic would be a mistake. Most frequent words follow predictable patterns, and many apparent irregularities make sense when we consider morphology or origin. Therefore, the most useful message for pupils is that English spelling is **a logical code with some exceptions**, and that these exceptions can be learned.

## 4. Proposals for teaching the written code

Given these characteristics, how should we teach the written code? This takes us to the third part of the title.

### 4.1. Approaches to teaching reading and spelling

Traditionally, three main approaches have been proposed. The **whole-word** or **look-and-say** approach teaches pupils to recognise words as visual units, often with flashcards. The **analytic phonics** approach starts from known words and analyses their parts, for instance noticing that *cat*, *hat* and *mat* share the ending *-at*. Finally, **synthetic phonics** explicitly teaches the correspondences between graphemes and phonemes and shows pupils how to **blend** them to read (*s-a-t* → *sat*) and to **segment** words into sounds to spell them.

### 4.2. What the evidence says

Research offers a clear answer about the early stages. The **National Reading Panel (2000)** concluded that **systematic phonics instruction** is more effective than unsystematic or no phonics teaching, especially in the first years. In England, the **Rose (2006)** review recommended **systematic synthetic phonics** as the main approach to early reading. More recently, **Castles, Rastle and Nation (2018)** have confirmed that explicit teaching of letter-sound relationships is essential at the beginning, while vocabulary and wide reading become increasingly important later.

### 4.3. Phonics adapted to English as a foreign language

Nevertheless, these studies were carried out mainly with native speakers, so phonics must be **adapted** to the foreign language classroom. Firstly, pupils should work with **words they already know orally**, as **Cameron (2001)** recommends, otherwise decoding produces sounds without meaning. Secondly, teachers should distinguish between **letter names** and **letter sounds**, because Spanish pupils tend to apply Spanish values to English letters. Thirdly, a **limited and progressive** set of patterns should be taught, starting with single sounds and moving to digraphs and the split digraph. Finally, very frequent **"tricky words"** that do not follow the patterns, such as *the*, *said* or *was*, should be learned as whole words.

**Example (2nd year).** Every week, the class learns one sound with a song and a gesture, such as /s/ with a snake movement. Pupils find objects in a box that start with that sound, read simple words by blending (*s-u-n*), and finally write the words under pictures. Since the words come from the unit's songs, pupils already know their meaning.

## 5. Orthographic applications in written productions

However, knowing the code is not enough: pupils must also apply it when they write, which is the final part of the title.

### 5.1. Spelling strategies

There are several effective strategies for learning spelling. The **look-say-cover-write-check** routine helps pupils to memorise words actively. **Word families** and **analogy** allow them to spell new words from known ones: if they can write *light*, they can write *night*, *fight* and *right*. **Morphology** helps too: knowing that *teacher* is *teach + er* explains its spelling. **Mnemonics** help with difficult words; for instance, *"because: big elephants can always understand small elephants"*. Finally, **spelling games**, such as hangman, word searches, spelling bees or building words with letter cards, make practice enjoyable.

### 5.2. Explicit instruction and feedback

Moreover, research supports teaching spelling explicitly. The meta-analysis by **Graham and Santangelo (2014)** found that **explicit, systematic spelling instruction** improves pupils' spelling, phonological awareness and reading, and that these gains transfer to spelling in their own texts. However, it did not significantly improve the overall quality of longer texts, which also requires teaching composition. Therefore, spelling should be taught explicitly but always connected to meaningful writing.

When correcting pupils' texts, teachers should **prioritise** high-frequency words and the patterns already taught, and encourage **self-correction**, for example by underlining a word and writing a code such as *Sp* so that pupils find the correct form themselves. A personal list of **"my tricky words"** helps each pupil to focus on their own difficulties.

**Example (4th year).** Pupils write a short description of their bedroom. Before writing, they review the *ee/ea* words of the unit (*sheets*, *clean*). After writing, they check their spelling with a partner using a word wall, correct the underlined words and add two of them to their personal spelling list.

## 6. Attention to diversity and assessment

Of course, an opaque orthography makes reading and spelling especially difficult for some pupils. Following **Universal Design for Learning** (**CAST, 2018**) and **Decree 85/2018**, pupils with **dyslexia** benefit from systematic, multisensory phonics, oral priority, audio support, fewer spelling words with more repetition and **no penalty for spelling** when assessing content. Newly arrived pupils may need extra work on sound-letter values, and pupils with high ability can investigate word origins or spelling rules.

Likewise, according to **Order 185/2022**, assessment should be **continuous and formative**. Instead of relying only on weekly spelling tests, teachers should assess **spelling in context**, observing how pupils apply the patterns in their own texts, and use self-assessment checklists that encourage pupils to review their work.

## 7. Conclusion

To conclude, let us return to the pupil who wrote *"I have a frend. He is very funy"*. That pupil was not careless, but logical: they applied the Spanish principle of writing what they hear. As we have seen, English spelling is **opaque** for historical reasons, but it is also a **logical code** with patterns that can be taught explicitly through **systematic phonics**, spelling strategies and meaningful writing. Our challenge as teachers is to make this code visible and enjoyable, so that every pupil learns to write not only what they hear, but what English readers expect to see.

> ✅ **Key points to remember**
> - 26 letters, ~44 phonemes; English is opaque, Spanish transparent (**Seymour et al., 2003**).
> - Why: printing before the Great Vowel Shift, borrowings, etymological spellings, silent letters (**Baugh & Cable, 2013**); American spelling (**Webster, 1828**).
> - Patterns: digraphs, split digraph, silent letters, doubling, morphological spelling (*-ed*).
> - Systematic phonics works (**National Reading Panel, 2000**; **Rose, 2006**; **Castles et al., 2018**); in EFL: known words first, letter sounds, tricky words (**Cameron, 2001**).
> - Explicit spelling instruction improves spelling and reading, not text quality on its own (**Graham & Santangelo, 2014**).
